How to convert PDF to Word on Mac

The file you want to edit is a PDF. The file you need is a Word document — a .docx you can open in Word, Pages or Google Docs and actually change. On a Mac there is no single built-in button for that job, but there are four honest ways to do it: in the browser with nothing to install, in Microsoft Word for Mac, free in Google Docs, or by copying the text out of Preview. Here is each one, and which to pick.

What your Mac can and cannot do

macOS is good at opening PDFs — Preview displays them beautifully, and Quick Look shows any PDF with a press of the Space bar. But opening is not converting. Preview's File > Export menu offers image formats and PDF, and nothing else: Preview cannot save a document as a Word file. Apple Pages cannot help either — it can open a Word document, but it cannot open a PDF as an editable document. And macOS has no built-in PDF-to-Word converter hiding in Automator or Shortcuts that does this job properly.

So the conversion has to happen somewhere else: in your browser, in Microsoft Word, or in Google Docs. All four methods below start with the same file — your PDF — and end with the same thing — an editable .docx. What differs is what they cost, what they upload, and how much formatting survives. Before you start, check which kind of PDF you have: open it in Preview, try to select a sentence, and copy it. If you can select and copy real text, it is a text PDF and any method below will work. If the whole page selects as one big image, or nothing selects at all, it is a scan — skip to the OCR section, because no converter can extract text that is not there. Our guide on how to convert PDF to Word without losing formatting explains why a PDF is hard to convert in the first place.

Option 1: convert in the browser (fastest, nothing to install)

If you need the document now, the browser method is the quickest: no app to install, no account, no payment, and it works on any Mac — including a borrowed or office Mac where you cannot install software.

  1. Open the PDF to Word tool. It opens straight in your browser.
  2. Choose your PDF. Text-based PDFs convert directly; the tool reads the text page by page and rebuilds it as a genuine .docx file.
  3. Download the .docx and open it in Word, Pages or Google Docs. Check the first page against the original PDF side by side before editing anything.
  4. Do the five-minute tidy-up below before you start writing — fixing structure first is much faster than fixing it around new edits.

Because the conversion runs in your browser, the document never leaves your Mac — which is the point of this method for contracts, CVs and anything personal. Most converters upload your file to a server; this one does not, and our guide on why browser-based tools are safer for personal documents explains what that changes.

Option 2: Microsoft Word for Mac

If Word is already installed on your Mac, it can do the conversion itself — with one honest catch. Open Microsoft Word, choose File > Open (or press Command + O), select your PDF, and confirm when Word says it will convert the file into an editable document. Then choose File > Save As, set the format to Word Document (.docx), and save.

The catch is that Word for Mac does the conversion through Microsoft's online service, not on your Mac: it needs an internet connection, a Microsoft 365 subscription or a one-time Word purchase, and your document is uploaded to Microsoft to be converted. For ordinary documents that is fine, but skip this method for anything your company or a client says must stay on your Mac. Microsoft itself says conversion works best with documents that are mostly text, and warns that complex layouts, tables and footnotes may not convert exactly.

Option 3: Google Docs (free)

No Word on your Mac? Google Docs converts PDFs for free, in any Mac browser. Upload the PDF to Google Drive, right-click (or Control-click) it, choose Open with > Google Docs, and wait while Docs reads the file. Then choose File > Download > Microsoft Word (.docx) and save the result.

The result is usually good for text-heavy documents, and the price is right — but the file is uploaded to Google, so the same privacy rule applies as with Word: fine for ordinary documents, not the choice for confidential ones. One practical tip: Google Docs is also the easiest way to fix a conversion that went wrong elsewhere, because you can edit the text in Docs and download a fresh .docx in seconds.

Option 4: copy and paste from Preview

For a short, simple document — a one-page letter, a form with a few paragraphs — you may not need a converter at all. Open the PDF in Preview, press Command + A to select all the text on the page (or drag to select just what you need), copy it, and paste it into a blank Word or Pages document. Then save or export as .docx.

This is honest, free and instant — and it is also the roughest method. Paste brings the words but not the layout: headings, tables and images all need rebuilding by hand, and page breaks land wherever they land. Use it for short documents only; for anything longer than a page or two, one of the methods above saves more time than it costs.

Scanned PDFs need OCR first

If your PDF is a scan, no converter can extract text from it, because there is no text — only pixels. The step before conversion is OCR: software that reads the words out of the image. Our Image to Text (OCR) tool extracts text from photos and screenshots in your browser, which gives you editable text to paste into Word. Expect to proofread carefully: OCR misreads similar letters (rn for m, 0 for O), struggles with small print and faded pages, and handles handwriting poorly. For a clean, modern scan, accuracy is usually above 95% — good enough to edit, not good enough to trust blindly.

One practical tip: if you have the choice, scan at 300 DPI or higher before running OCR. Low-resolution scans are the single biggest cause of OCR errors, and no amount of clever software recovers detail that was never captured.

The five-minute tidy-up after conversion

Almost every converted document needs the same small fixes, whichever method you used. Work through them in this order:

  • Headings first. Select each heading and apply the proper Heading 1, 2 or 3 style. This single step restores the document outline, lets you generate a table of contents, and fixes most of the odd spacing.
  • Tables next. Check that merged cells and column widths survived. A table that arrived as loose text can usually be rebuilt in seconds with Insert → Table → Convert Text to Table.
  • Images and captions. Pictures usually arrive in roughly the right place; set their text wrapping to "Square" or "Top and Bottom" and they will stop jumping around as you edit.
  • Headers, footers and page numbers. These often land in the body text. Delete the stray copies and reinsert them properly with Insert → Header or Page Number.
  • Line breaks and hyphens. Turn on Show/Hide (¶) and remove manual line breaks inside paragraphs, and search for hyphens at line ends where words were split across lines in the PDF.

That is the whole job for most documents. A ten-page report typically takes five to ten minutes; a complex forty-page proposal might take half an hour — still a fraction of the hours retyping would cost.

Which method should you choose?

Pick the browser tool when the document is private or you need it now — nothing is installed and nothing is uploaded. Pick Word for Mac when Word is already on your Mac and the document is ordinary — it keeps the most formatting of the desktop options, but it uploads the file to Microsoft. Pick Google Docs when you have no Word and the document is not confidential — it is free and surprisingly good with plain text. Pick copy-and-paste only for short, simple documents. Adobe Acrobat (paid) is the remaining option for complex files you convert every week. And if the PDF is a scan, start with OCR, whichever route you choose afterwards.

One last rule, whichever method you pick: work on a copy, and keep the original PDF. The PDF is your reference for what the document should look like — never convert the only copy of anything.

Questions people ask

Can Preview convert a PDF to Word on Mac?

No. Preview opens PDFs and lets you copy text out of them, but its File > Export menu offers only image formats and PDF — there is no Word (.docx) option. To get an editable Word document you need a converter: a browser tool, Word for Mac, or Google Docs.

What is the free way to convert PDF to Word on Mac?

Two free ways: convert in the browser with our PDF to Word tool, which needs no install and never uploads your file; or upload the PDF to Google Drive, open it with Google Docs, and download it as Microsoft Word (.docx). Google Docs is free with a Google account, but the file is uploaded to Google.

Will my formatting survive the conversion?

Most of it, if the PDF is a clean text document: paragraphs, headings and bold text usually survive, while multi-column layouts, complex tables and exact spacing need a few minutes of tidying afterwards. A PDF stores where letters sit, not what they mean, so no converter preserves everything — expect a very good starting point, then the five-minute tidy-up in this guide.

What should I do with a scanned PDF on Mac?

A scanned PDF is a set of photographs of pages, so there is no text to convert — run OCR (optical character recognition) first to read the words out of the images. Our Image to Text tool extracts text from photos and screenshots in the browser, which gives you editable text you can paste into Word. Expect to proofread the result.
