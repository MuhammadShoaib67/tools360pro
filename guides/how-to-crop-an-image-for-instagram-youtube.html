How to crop an image for Instagram, YouTube and more (free)

Every platform shows your photo in a different frame — square on Instagram, wide on YouTube, tall on stories — and if the shape is wrong, the platform crops it for you, usually badly. Here is how to crop an image for Instagram, YouTube and the rest yourself, with the right size for each place, free in your browser.

Why every platform wants a different shape

A photograph is a rectangle, but the frame it lands in is decided by the platform, not by you. Instagram's feed is built around squares and tall portraits, YouTube thumbnails are wide, stories fill a phone screen from top to bottom, and a profile photo is shown as a small circle. Upload a wide landscape photo as a profile picture and the platform keeps only the middle slice — often cutting off the very person the photo is of.

Cropping before you upload is how you stay in control. You choose what stays in the frame and what goes, instead of trusting an automatic crop that cannot tell a face from a fence. It takes less than a minute, and it is the difference between a post that looks deliberate and one that looks accidentally chopped.

The sizes that matter

These are the sizes the platforms themselves recommend. You do not need to memorise them — keep this list open while you crop, and match the shape to the place the image will live.

  • Instagram square post: 1:1, 1080 × 1080 pixels.
  • Instagram portrait post: 4:5, 1080 × 1350 pixels — the tallest shape the feed allows, and the one that takes up the most screen.
  • Instagram story or reel cover: 9:16, 1080 × 1920 pixels — a full phone screen.
  • Instagram profile photo: square, at least 320 × 320 pixels — it is shown as a small circle, so keep the face in the centre.
  • YouTube thumbnail: 16:9, 1280 × 720 pixels.
  • YouTube profile photo: square, 800 × 800 pixels — also shown as a circle.
  • YouTube channel banner: 2560 × 1440 pixels, with the important content inside the middle 1546 × 423 safe area, which is all most screens show.
  • Facebook profile photo: square, shown at 170 × 170 pixels on desktop; Facebook cover photo: 820 × 312 pixels.
  • X (Twitter) header: 1500 × 500 pixels, a wide 3:1 banner.
  • LinkedIn profile photo: square, 400 × 400 pixels; LinkedIn banner: 1584 × 396 pixels.

Two numbers matter for each entry: the ratio (the shape) and the pixel size (the resolution). The ratio decides what fits in the frame; the pixel size decides how sharp it looks. A 1:1 crop at 300 × 300 pixels is the right shape for an Instagram post but far too small — it will look soft when the app shows it at 1080 pixels wide. Always crop from the largest original you have.

Cropping for Instagram: square, portrait and stories

Instagram is where cropping matters most, because one photo can end up in three different frames. A square post uses the 1:1 preset at 1080 × 1080. A portrait post — the shape that fills the most of a follower's screen — is 4:5 at 1080 × 1350; there is no 4:5 preset, so use Free mode and type those numbers in the width and height fields. A story or reel cover is 9:16 at 1080 × 1920, again with Free mode and exact pixels.

Two details people miss: first, Instagram shows profile photos as a circle, so a face near the edge of a square crop gets cut off — keep faces and logos in the centre third. Second, stories hide the top and bottom edges behind the app's own interface, so keep text and faces away from the outer 10% of a 9:16 crop. Crop once for the shape, then check the result at the size it will actually be seen — a detail that looks fine on a laptop can vanish on a phone.

Cropping for YouTube: thumbnails and profile photos

A YouTube thumbnail is 16:9 at 1280 × 720, and it is usually seen at a fraction of that size — in search results and on the side of a video, a thumbnail may be only a few hundred pixels wide. That changes how you crop: the subject needs to be large in the frame, the background simple, and nothing important near the edges, because the bottom-right corner is covered by the video's duration badge.

Use the 16:9 preset, drag the box over the subject, and leave a little breathing room around faces and text. For the channel profile photo, use the 1:1 preset at 800 × 800 and centre the face or logo — YouTube crops it to a circle everywhere it appears, including in comments. If you also need a channel banner, the shape is 2560 × 1440 with a safe area of 1546 × 423 in the middle; crop wide and keep the important content in that centre band, since phones and TVs show different amounts of the edges.

What cropping cannot fix

Cropping changes the frame, not the photo. It cannot sharpen a blurry image, add detail that was never captured, or rescue a photo taken in bad light. A crop that leaves only a small corner of the original and then gets stretched to fill a 1920-pixel story will look soft, because those few pixels are being asked to cover a large screen — the fix is to start from a higher-resolution original, not to crop harder.

It also cannot change the ratio of the pixels themselves. Turning a wide photo into a tall story means cutting away the sides; if the subject is spread across the whole width, no crop will keep all of it. In that case the honest options are a different shape (a square post instead of a story), or a different photo. And remember that cropping is destructive in one sense: the pixels outside the box are gone from the downloaded file, so keep your original safe and crop a copy when the photo matters.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Cropping a screenshot instead of the original. Screenshots are already compressed and lower resolution; crop the original photo whenever you still have it.
  • Letting the platform crop for you. An automatic crop centres the frame, which is wrong whenever the subject is off-centre. Spend the minute and crop it yourself.
  • Putting faces or text at the edges. Circles, badges and interface overlays all eat the edges — keep the important parts in the centre.
  • Cropping, then cropping the crop. Every round of editing from an already-edited file loses a little quality. Always go back to the original for a new shape.
  • Forgetting the file size. A cropped photo can still be several megabytes. If the platform complains or the upload is slow, run it through our Compress Image tool after cropping — crop first, compress second, never the other way round.

Questions people ask

What size should I crop a photo for Instagram?

A square post is 1:1 at 1080 by 1080 pixels, a portrait post is 4:5 at 1080 by 1350, and a story or reel is 9:16 at 1080 by 1920. Crop to the shape of the place the photo will live, not to a size you guess — the platform will crop it for you if you do not, and its automatic crop is rarely the one you would choose.

How do I crop a photo for a YouTube thumbnail?

A YouTube thumbnail is 16:9 at 1280 by 720 pixels. Use the 16:9 preset in our Crop Image tool, drag the box over the part of the photo that carries the subject, and download. Keep faces and any text well inside the frame, because YouTube shows thumbnails at small sizes where the edges are easy to miss.

Does cropping reduce image quality?

Cropping itself does not blur anything: the pixels you keep are the same pixels from the original, at the same resolution. Quality only suffers when the crop leaves you with too few pixels for the job — for example, cutting a small corner out of a photo and then stretching it to fill a 1920-pixel story. Start from the largest original you have and crop before you resize or compress.

Can I crop a photo to 9:16 for a story with this tool?
