How to calculate percentage (the easy way)

By Muhammad Shoaib · Published 22 November 2026 · Updated 22 November 2026

A price is down 25%, a tip should be 15%, a test score is 42 out of 50 — and the question underneath is always the same: how do I calculate a percentage? The good news is that almost every percentage question is one of three, and each one has a single short formula. Here is how to calculate percentage the easy way, with worked examples you can copy, and our free calculator for the rest.

The one idea behind every percentage

A percentage is simply a part out of a hundred. The word itself says so: per cent means per hundred. So 25% means 25 out of 100, which is the same as the fraction 25/100 and the decimal 0.25. Every percentage calculation is a rearrangement of that one idea — find the part, find the whole, and scale it to a hundred.

The most useful move is turning a percentage into a decimal: divide by 100. 20% becomes 0.20, 7.5% becomes 0.075, 150% becomes 1.5. Once a percentage is a decimal, it multiplies like any other number — which is why "20% of 150" is just 0.20 × 150 = 30. Keep that conversion in mind and the three questions below stop looking like three different kinds of maths and start looking like one idea used three ways.

Question 1: What is X% of Y?

This is the discount, tax and tip question, and it is the one people meet most often. The formula is short: multiply Y by X and divide by 100.

What is 20% of 150? 150 × 20 ÷ 100 = 30. So 20% of 150 is 30.

A 25% discount on an 80 item: 80 × 25 ÷ 100 = 20, so the saving is 20 and the price after discount is 60. A 15% tip on a 40 bill: 40 × 15 ÷ 100 = 6, so the tip is 6. The pattern never changes — the percentage tells you how many parts out of a hundred, and the multiplication does the rest.

Question 2: X is what percent of Y?

This is the exam-marks and survey-results question: you have two numbers and want to know one as a percentage of the other. The formula is the first one run backwards: divide X by Y and multiply by 100.

What is 30 as a percent of 120? 30 ÷ 120 × 100 = 25%. So 30 is 25% of 120.

A test score of 42 out of 50: 42 ÷ 50 × 100 = 84%. 18 correct out of 20 questions: 18 ÷ 20 × 100 = 90%. The same formula answers "what share of the whole is this part" wherever it appears — a budget spent, a survey answered, a target reached.

One detail worth getting right: the second number, Y, is the whole, the 100% you are measuring against. Mixing the two numbers up is the usual mistake — 30 as a percent of 120 is 25%, but 120 as a percent of 30 is 400%, a very different answer from the same two numbers. Question 3: Percentage increase or decrease

This is the growth question: how much has something moved relative to where it started? The formula is: subtract the starting value from the new value, divide by the starting value, then multiply by 100.

A price rises from 80 to 100: (100 − 80) ÷ 80 × 100 = 25% increase. A price falls from 80 to 60: (60 − 80) ÷ 80 × 100 = −25%, a 25% decrease. A salary moves from 50,000 to 55,000: (55,000 − 50,000) ÷ 50,000 × 100 = 10% increase.

The part people get wrong most often is the starting value. Percentage change is always measured against where the number started, so the same absolute move means a different percentage depending on the start. A rise of 25 is a 50% increase from 50, but only a 25% increase from 100. And a measurement that doubles has risen 100% — not 200%, a slip that is easy to make in conversation. Percent vs percentage points — the classic mix-up

One of the most common percentage mistakes is mixing up percentage points and percent, and it shows up constantly in news about elections, inflation and interest rates. If an interest rate rises from 4% to 5%, it has risen by one percentage point — but in relative terms it has risen by 25%, because 1 is a quarter of 4. Both numbers describe the same rise; they just measure it differently.

The rule is: percentage points describe the absolute difference between two percentages, while percent describes the relative change. A poll moving from 40% to 44% has moved four percentage points, which is a 10% relative rise. Reading a headline correctly means knowing which of the two is being quoted — and now that you know the difference, you will spot the mix-up every time someone else makes it.

Percentages in everyday money

Most percentage maths in daily life is money maths, and it is almost always question 1 or question 3 in disguise. A discount is question 1: 25% off 80 is a saving of 20. The same panel works in reverse for a price already reduced: if a 100 item is now selling for 75, question 2 says 75 is 75% of 100, so the discount you actually got was 25%.

Tax works the same way. A bill that includes tax can be unwound by finding what the pre-tax amount was, which is the same "percent of" calculation run backwards. And a salary negotiation is question 3: a rise from 50,000 to 55,000 is a 10% increase, while a rise from 40,000 to 44,000 is the same 10% — the absolute amounts differ, but the relative move is identical, which is why percentages are the fair way to compare them.

None of this needs memorising three separate formulas if you remember the one idea: a percentage is a part out of a hundred. The calculator covers the arithmetic; the understanding is what stops a wrong number from slipping through. When a result looks surprising, check it by hand — the next section shows how quick that is.

Check your answer by hand in ten seconds

The calculator shows its formula for a reason: a quick check by hand catches typos before they matter. What is 20% of 50? Twenty percent is 20 out of 100, so 20 ÷ 100 × 50 = 10. What is 30 as a percent of 120? Divide 30 by 120 and multiply by 100 to get 25%. If your hand check and the calculator disagree, re-read the numbers you typed — a misplaced decimal is the usual culprit.

Round numbers make good checks. 10% of anything is that thing divided by 10, so 10% of 240 is 24 — and 20% must be double that, 48, while 5% must be half, 12. If a discount calculation gives a number that does not sit sensibly between those landmarks, something is off. That ten-second habit is worth more than any calculator, because it is the habit that catches the error.
