How to resize an image without losing quality

Most resized images go soft for a reason that has nothing to do with the resize button: people resize a copy of a copy, stretch the proportions, or save a photograph in the wrong format. Resizing itself is honest work — it changes how many pixels an image has. Do it once, from the original, with the right settings, and a smaller image can look just as sharp as the one you started with.

What resizing actually does to an image

An image is a grid of pixels. A photo from a modern phone might be 4000 by 3000 pixels — twelve million tiny squares of colour. Resizing changes the size of that grid. Making it smaller means the software must merge neighbouring pixels into fewer ones; making it bigger means it must invent new pixels between the existing ones. The two directions behave very differently, and that difference is the whole story of quality.

When you shrink an image, the software averages groups of pixels together. A 4000-pixel-wide photo reduced to 1000 pixels still has every part of the scene in it — just described with fewer squares. At the size it will actually be shown, the result usually looks identical to the original, because a screen cannot show more detail than its own pixels allow. When you enlarge an image, there is nothing to average: the software must guess what the missing pixels should be. That guessing is why enlargements go soft, no matter which tool you use.

Downscaling: why smaller usually stays sharp

Downscaling is the safe direction. A photograph shown at 1080 pixels wide needs only 1080 pixels of information, so a 4000-pixel original reduced to 1080 loses nothing the viewer could have seen. This is why professional websites resize every uploaded photo: the full-size original is wasted weight on a page that displays it at a fraction of its size.

There is one condition, and it matters more than any setting: always resize from the largest original you have. Every resize throws away information, so resizing a file that was already resized compounds the loss. Keep the original untouched on your device, make each resized version from it, and the result stays as sharp as the source allows. A second condition is the aspect ratio — the width-to-height proportion. Keep it locked while you resize, or the picture stretches and faces and circles distort. Stretching is the most visible quality mistake of all, and it is entirely avoidable.

Upscaling: the hard limit nobody can beat

Enlarging an image cannot add detail. A 500-pixel-wide logo stretched to 2000 pixels wide has the same information spread over sixteen times as many pixels, so edges blur and fine texture smooths out. Good resizing software softens this with interpolation — it blends neighbouring pixels so the result looks smooth rather than blocky — but smooth is not the same as sharp. AI upscalers can do better by recognising what is in the picture and rebuilding plausible detail, yet even they are guessing; they cannot recover text or texture that was never captured.

The honest rule is simple: a modest upscale of a sharp, high-resolution original — up to about twice its size — is usually acceptable on screen. Beyond that, the softness becomes obvious. If a picture must be shown large, the fix is not a better enlarger; it is a bigger original. Re-take the photo, ask for the original file, or accept a smaller display size. The method: resize without losing quality

  1. Start from the original file. Find the largest, earliest version of the image — the one straight from the camera or the designer — and resize that. Never resize a file that was already resized, compressed for a chat app, or downloaded from a social feed.
  2. Decide the exact size you need. A profile photo, a form upload and a blog banner all want different dimensions. Guessing wastes quality: resizing to 800 pixels and then to 400 is two rounds of loss where one would do. Common sizes are listed below.
  3. Resize in the browser. Open the Resize Image tool, drop the image in, and type the width and height in pixels — or use a percentage. Keep the aspect-ratio lock on unless you deliberately want a different shape, and use the preview to compare the result with the original before downloading.
  4. Choose the right format and quality. For photographs, JPG or WebP at around 90–92% quality keeps the picture sharp at a fraction of the original size. For graphics with flat colours and text, PNG stays crisp. WebP is usually the smallest of the three for the same visible quality.
  5. Check the result at the size it will be used. Open the resized file and view it at 100% — the size it will actually appear. If it looks clean there, it is done. Zooming far beyond its real display size will always show softness, because you are looking at pixels that were never meant to be seen that large.

Common sizes, so you resize once

These are the dimensions most platforms expect. Type them straight into the width and height boxes:

  • Instagram post: 1080 × 1080 pixels (square).
  • YouTube thumbnail: 1280 × 720 pixels.
  • X (Twitter) post image: 1600 × 900 pixels.
  • Facebook post image: 1200 × 630 pixels.
  • Website hero or banner: 1920 pixels wide is a safe maximum; most screens never show more.
  • Email or form upload: 800–1200 pixels on the long edge is usually plenty, and keeps the file small.

If the platform crops your image to a different shape — Instagram stories are vertical, for example — resizing alone will not fix the shape. That is a cropping job, not a resizing job: use our Crop Image tool to frame the picture first, then resize the cropped result to the platform's dimensions.

Resize first, compress second

Resizing and compressing are two different jobs, and the order matters. Resizing removes pixels; compression removes data from the pixels that remain. Resize first, and most of the file weight disappears with the pixels you no longer need. Compress second, and the compressor only has to work on the image you will actually use.

Doing it the other way round wastes effort twice over: you compress detail that resizing is about to throw away, and then you compress the resized result again. Mistakes that quietly ruin quality

The most common is resizing a resized copy — each round throws away a little more, and three rounds of small losses add up to a visibly soft image. Keep the original and always work from it. The second is stretching: changing width and height independently distorts everything in the frame, so keep the aspect-ratio lock on unless the shape change is deliberate. The third is saving photographs as PNG. PNG is lossless, which sounds better, but for a photograph it produces an enormous file with no visible benefit over a high-quality JPG — and some platforms then compress that huge PNG themselves, badly. The fourth is the reverse: saving a logo or a screenshot with text as a low-quality JPG, which smears the sharp edges that PNG would have kept clean. Match the format to the picture, not to habit.

Finally, do not judge a resized image by zooming into it at 300%. Every digital image falls apart under enough magnification. Judge it at the size it will be shown — on the page, in the post, in the form — because that is the only size that matters.

Questions people ask

Can I resize an image without any quality loss?

Making an image smaller (downscaling) keeps it sharp as long as you start from the original file, keep the aspect ratio locked, and save at a sensible quality setting — around 90% for JPG or WebP. The loss people notice almost always comes from resizing an already-resized copy, stretching the image, or saving a photo as PNG when JPG would be smaller and cleaner.

How much can I enlarge an image before it looks bad?

Enlarging never adds detail that was not in the original — the software has to guess the missing pixels, so the result gets softer the further you go. A modest upscale to about 200% of a sharp, high-resolution original is usually acceptable on screen; beyond that, edges blur and textures turn smooth. If a picture must be shown large, start from the biggest original you have rather than enlarging a small copy.

Should I resize or compress an image first?

Resize first, then compress. Resizing to the size you actually need removes most of the pixels — and most of the file weight — before compression even starts. Compressing first and resizing second means you compress detail that is about to be thrown away, and you risk compressing twice. If a form asks for an exact file size, resize to the right dimensions first, then use a compressor to hit the target kilobytes.
