Citations without the panic: APA 7th vs MLA 9th

Citations feel harder than they are, because the two styles students meet most — APA 7th and MLA 9th — agree on the big idea and disagree on almost every detail. Once you know which details each style actually checks, the panic goes away: a citation is just a small, fixed recipe, and the same recipe works every time.

What a citation actually does

A citation does two jobs at once. Inside your text, a short bracket tells the reader whose idea this is and where to find it. At the end of your paper, the full entry gives them everything needed to track the source down. That is all. It is not decoration, and it is not a test of memory — it is a map, and a marker reads it to check that your claims stand on real sources rather than on assertion.

The two styles exist because different subjects care about different things. Psychology, education and the sciences use APA, where the date of a study matters — a 2019 finding and a 2024 finding can say opposite things, so the year sits right next to the author's name. Literature, languages and the humanities use MLA, where the exact page matters — a quotation lives on page 47, not in a year, so the page number is what follows the name.

APA vs MLA at a glance

In-text citation. APA uses author and date: (Khan, 2023). MLA uses author and page: (Khan 47). That single difference explains most of the confusion — if your brackets contain a year, you are writing APA; if they contain a page number, you are writing MLA.

The list at the end. APA calls it "References" and sorts entries by the author's surname. MLA calls it "Works Cited" and does the same. Both use a hanging indent — the first line of each entry starts at the margin and later lines are indented — which is why reference lists look the way they do.

Titles. APA writes article and chapter titles in sentence case: only the first word and proper nouns are capitalised, while book and journal titles use title case and italics. MLA writes all titles in title case, with articles and short works in quotation marks and books and journals in italics.

Dates and DOIs. APA always shows the year prominently and adds a DOI or URL for online sources, written as a full link. MLA puts the date near the end of the entry, and shows the DOI or URL without the http:// or https:// prefix. For a print book, neither style needs a link at all.

Author names. APA lists up to 20 authors in a reference entry before using an ellipsis; in the text, three or more authors become "et al." from the first citation. MLA lists the first author followed by "et al." for three or more authors in both places, and writes authors' full first names where APA uses initials only.

The five mistakes markers see most

  • A source in the text but not in the list — or the reverse. Every in-text citation must have a matching entry at the end, and every entry must be cited somewhere. A mismatch is the single most common error.
  • Guessing the style. Writing (Khan, 2023, p. 47) mixes both styles at once. Pick one and stay in it for the whole paper.
  • Capitalising titles the wrong way. APA sentence case against MLA title case is the detail markers spot first, because it appears in every single entry.
  • Forgetting the hanging indent. It looks like a formatting quirk, but it is part of both styles — entries without it read as unfinished.
  • Citing a source you did not read. Quoting a study you found quoted in someone else's paper, and citing the original as if you read it, is how errors spread. Cite the paper you actually read, and say "as cited in" when you must rely on a second-hand source.

A calm method: build citations as you write

  1. Decide the style before the first paragraph. Check the assignment brief; if it does not name a style, ask. Guessing halfway through means reformatting everything.
  2. Record each source the moment you use it. Author, year, title, journal or publisher, pages, and the DOI or URL — written down while the tab is still open. Hunting for a lost URL at midnight is how citations go wrong.
  3. Generate the formatted entry with a tool. Our Citation Generator builds APA 7th, MLA 9th and Chicago entries from the details you paste in, so you can check the punctuation instead of memorising it.
  4. Check the list against the text. Before submitting, read through your paper once with only the citations in mind: does every bracket have a matching entry, and does every entry appear in the text?
  5. Check the length too. A paper that is supposed to be 1,500 words is usually judged on the body text, not the reference list — use our Word Counter to confirm the count before you submit.

One last reassurance: nobody expects you to memorise either style. Professional researchers use reference managers for exactly this reason. What markers expect is consistency — one style, applied the same way from the first citation to the last. Questions people ask

Can I mix APA and MLA in one paper?

No — pick the style your teacher or department asked for and use it for everything: in-text citations, the list at the end, headings and title capitalisation. Mixing styles is one of the fastest ways to lose marks, because it tells the marker you were guessing. If no style was specified, ask before you start writing, not the night before the deadline.

Do I need a citation for common knowledge?

No. Facts that any educated reader would already know — that water boils at 100°C at sea level, that Shakespeare wrote Hamlet — do not need a source. The test is whether the claim could reasonably be challenged or traced to a particular study: an opinion, a statistic, a quotation and any specific finding always need a citation, even if the fact feels obvious to you.

What if my source has no author or no date?

Use what the source does give you. In APA, a missing author means the title moves into the author position, and a missing date becomes (n.d.). In MLA, a missing author means the entry starts with the title, and the date is simply left out. Never invent an author or a year to fill the gap — a marker checks, and a guessed name is worse than an honest gap.

Do URLs and DOIs go in every reference?

In APA 7th, yes for online sources: include a DOI when one exists (as a link, https://doi.org/...) and otherwise the URL of the page you used. In MLA 9th, include the DOI or URL as well, but drop the http:// or https:// prefix. For a print book or a journal article you read in print, neither style needs a link at all.
