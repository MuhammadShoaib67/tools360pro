How to compress a PDF without losing quality

The honest answer: it depends on why your PDF is big. Scanned and photo-heavy PDFs can shrink a lot and still look sharp. Text documents are already efficient — and the wrong kind of compression can even make them bigger. Here is how to tell the difference, and what to do in each case.

Why PDFs get big in the first place

Almost every oversized PDF is big for one reason: images. A scanned page is a photograph, and a phone photograph of a document can be several megabytes on its own. A 30-page scanned report can easily pass 50 MB, while a 30-page report typed in Word and exported to PDF might be 200 KB. The text in a typed PDF is stored as characters — a few bytes per letter — but a scan stores every pixel of the page. That single difference decides which compression method will work and which will disappoint you.

Other, smaller causes exist too: embedded fonts, leftover editing data and uncompressed graphics inside files produced by design software. Those can be removed with no visible change at all, which is the only truly lossless compression there is.

The two kinds of compression

Lossless compression repacks the file without discarding anything: duplicate fonts are merged, streams are compressed, unused objects are dropped. The document looks identical because it is identical — the savings are real but modest, usually 5–20%.

Lossy compression shrinks the images inside the PDF — lowering their resolution and re-encoding them as compact JPEGs. This is where the big wins are (often 70–90% on scans), and it is also where quality can be lost if the settings are pushed too far. A scan captured at 300 DPI can usually be shown at 150 DPI and remain perfectly readable on screens and in printouts of normal documents; faces in photographs and fine print are what suffer first when you go lower.

A practical method that keeps PDFs sharp

  1. Check what kind of PDF you have. Zoom into a page. If you can select and copy the text, it is a text PDF — compress it only if it contains large photos. If the whole page behaves like one picture, it is a scan, and compression will work well.
  2. Remove pages you do not need first. Splitting out unwanted pages is lossless and often halves the file. Our Split PDF tool extracts or deletes page ranges without touching quality.
  3. Compress the scan or photo document. Use the Compress PDF tool — it re-renders pages as compact images, which is exactly right for scans. Because it runs in your browser, the document never leaves your device.
  4. Read the result before you send it. Open the compressed file and zoom to 100% on the smallest text. If it reads cleanly, you are done. If not, compress the original again with gentler settings rather than compressing an already-compressed copy.

When NOT to compress

Do not compress a PDF you need to print professionally, a document whose photos are the point (a portfolio, a medical scan), or any file you must archive in original form — keep the master untouched and compress a copy for sending. And if our tool ever reports that the result is not smaller, it hands you your original file back: a text PDF that is already compact should simply be sent as it is.

Compressing is one step in a common workflow: Merge PDF to combine documents into one, compress the merged file if the scans make it heavy, and use Images to PDF when the source material is a set of photos in the first place — choosing sensible image sizes there prevents the problem before it starts.

Questions people ask

Can a PDF be compressed with no quality loss at all?

Sometimes. If a PDF is large because it carries duplicate fonts, unused objects or uncompressed images, a lossless cleanup can shrink it with zero visual change. But most large PDFs are large because of their photos and scans — making those smaller always trades a little quality, and the honest goal is a file that still looks sharp at normal reading size, not a mathematically identical one.

Why did my text PDF get bigger after compressing?

Text PDFs are already compact: text is stored as characters, not pictures. Tools that re-render pages as images (including ours) replace that efficient text with pictures, which can grow the file. For text-heavy documents, keep the original or remove pages you do not need with a splitter instead of compressing.

What size should a PDF be for email?

Most email services accept attachments up to 20–25 MB, but large files are slow for the receiver. A good target is under 5 MB for a document people will open on phones, and under 1 MB when a form or portal asks for an upload — the autocomplete data shows 'compress pdf to 1mb' is one of the most common searches for exactly this reason.
