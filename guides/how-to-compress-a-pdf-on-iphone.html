How to compress a PDF on iPhone (free)

By Muhammad Shoaib · Published 21 November 2026 · Updated 21 November 2026

The PDF is ready, the form wants it under 5 MB, and the file on your iPhone is 24 MB. iOS has no built-in button for making a PDF smaller — and the one that looks like it does something else entirely. Here is how to compress a PDF on iPhone for free, in Safari, with no app to install and nothing uploaded.

Why iPhone PDFs get so big

Almost every oversized PDF on an iPhone is big for one reason: images. A PDF made from phone photographs — a scanned contract, a set of notes photographed page by page, a receipt saved from an app — carries a full photograph for every page, and a phone photograph can be several megabytes on its own. A 30-page scanned report can easily pass 50 MB, while a 30-page report typed in Word and exported to PDF might be 200 KB. The text in a typed PDF is stored as characters, a few bytes per letter, but a scan stores every pixel of the page.

That is also why the problem usually appears at the worst moment: a job portal, a university form, or an email attachment that refuses a file over its limit. The fix is to shrink the images inside the PDF, which is exactly what a PDF compressor does — and on an iPhone the fastest place to do it is Safari, not the App Store.

The Files app's Compress button does not do this

The trap most people fall into first is the Files app itself. Long-press a PDF in Files and the menu offers Compress — which sounds like the answer, but is not. What it makes is a ZIP archive containing the same PDF, byte for byte unchanged. The file you send is still the same size once unzipped, and the person receiving it has to unzip it before they can even open it. A portal that asks for a PDF under a size limit will still reject it, because the PDF inside the ZIP is still too big.

iOS simply has no built-in way to make a PDF itself smaller: no Reduce File Size command, no quality setting in the share sheet, nothing in Settings. The Mail app does offer to scale down photos when you attach them — Small, Medium, Large, or Actual Size — but that only works for image attachments, not for a PDF file. So the compression has to happen in a browser tool or a third-party app, and the browser tool is the quicker and more private of the two.

Option 1: compress in Safari (fastest, nothing to install)

This is the method to use first. It needs no app, no account, and no payment, and the file never leaves your phone — the compression runs inside the Safari tab itself.

  1. Open the Compress PDF tool in Safari. It opens straight in the browser.
  2. Tap the box and choose your PDF. Safari opens the Files picker, so you can pick the file from On My iPhone, iCloud Drive, or Downloads — wherever the PDF lives. The tool shows its size and page count.
  3. Pick a quality level: High quality, Balanced, or Small file. Balanced is the right default for most files; the next section explains when to choose the other two.
  4. Tap Compress PDF and wait while each page is re-rendered and rebuilt in your browser. On a phone this takes a few seconds for a short document and longer for a long scanned one.
  5. Compare the sizes, then download. The tool shows the original size next to the compressed size. Safari saves the smaller PDF to Files, in Downloads, ready to attach to an email or upload to a form.

One thing to know before you send the result: this kind of compression re-renders each page as a compact image, so the text in the compressed file is no longer selectable or searchable, and links, bookmarks, and form fields do not carry over. Keep your original PDF for anything you still need to edit or search, and send the compressed copy only. Which quality level should you pick?

The three levels are the same choice every time, and the right one depends on what happens to the file next — not on how big it is now.

  • High quality keeps pages closest to the originals and shrinks the file least. Pick it when someone will print the document or read it closely, and the size limit is generous.
  • Balanced is the everyday middle ground, and the right default for emailing a scanned letter, sharing notes, or uploading to a form with a 5 or 10 MB limit. Normal reading stays sharp.
  • Small file squeezes hardest and leaves pages softer. Pick it only when a form or portal enforces a strict limit — 1 or 2 MB — and the file must get under it whatever it takes.

Whatever level you pick, check the result before you send it: open the compressed file and zoom to 100% on the smallest text. If it reads cleanly, you are done. If it does not, compress the original again with a gentler level — never compress an already-compressed copy, because each round throws away more detail. And if the tool reports that the result is not smaller, it hands you your original file back: a text-only PDF is already compact, and no honest compressor can shrink it much further.

When compression will not help much

Compression works on images, so it works best on the files that are big because of images. A text-only PDF — a report exported from Word, a form filled in on a computer — is already compact, and compressing it may save only a few percent or nothing at all. That is not a failure of the tool; there is simply nothing to shrink.

When the file is big but compression barely moves it, the pages themselves are the problem, and removing pages is the fix. A 40-page document where only 12 pages matter becomes a much smaller file the moment the other 28 go: use our Split PDF tool in Safari to extract just the pages you need, then compress what is left. And if the PDF was made from photos in the first place, the smarter fix is upstream — our Images to PDF tool lets you choose sensible image sizes before the PDF is built, so the problem never starts.

What about compressor apps from the App Store?

The App Store offers dozens of PDF compressor apps, and most of them work — but they come with two costs the browser method does not have. First, they upload your file to their server to compress it, which means a contract, a medical form, or a bank statement leaves your phone and sits on a computer you do not control. Second, many of them are free only for one or two files a day, and then ask for a weekly or monthly subscription to continue.

If you do install one, read what it does with your files before you hand it anything personal, and check whether it deletes uploads afterwards. For a one-off file — a form, an application, a scanned letter — the Safari method is faster, free, and private, with nothing to install and nothing to cancel later. That is why it is the first option in this guide, and the only one most people will ever need.

Before you send it: the two-minute check

A compressed PDF is a new file, and it deserves a quick look before it goes anywhere important. Open it in the Files app and flip through it: every page present, in the right order, nothing cut off at the edges. Then zoom to 100% on the smallest text on the busiest page — a footnote, a signature, a table of numbers — and read it. If it reads cleanly at full size, it will read cleanly for whoever receives it. If it does not, go back to the original and compress again at a gentler level. Keep the original safe either way: compression is a one-way trip for image quality, and the original is the only copy that still has all of it.

Questions people ask

How do I make a PDF smaller on my iPhone?

Open our Compress PDF tool in Safari, tap the box, choose the PDF from the Files app, pick a quality level, and tap Compress PDF. The smaller file downloads to Files, in Downloads, ready to attach or upload. Nothing is installed and the file never leaves your phone.

Does the Files app compress a PDF on iPhone?

Not into a smaller PDF. Long-pressing a PDF in the Files app and choosing Compress makes a ZIP archive containing the same PDF — the PDF inside is unchanged, and the person receiving it has to unzip it first. iOS has no built-in way to make a PDF itself smaller; you need a compressor, and a browser tool in Safari does it without installing an app.

Will compressing a PDF on my iPhone make the text blurry?

It can, on scanned and photo-heavy pages, if you squeeze too hard — the Small file level re-encodes page images at low quality, and fine print softens first. Balanced keeps normal reading sharp in almost every case, and High quality stays closest to the original. Open the result and zoom to 100 percent on the smallest text before you send it; if it reads cleanly, you are done.

Is it safe to compress a PDF in Safari on my iPhone?

With our tool, yes: the compression runs inside your browser tab, so the document is never uploaded to a server and never stored anywhere else. Most App Store compressor apps, by contrast, upload your file to their server and many ask for a subscription — check what an app does with your file before you hand it a contract, a medical form, or anything personal.
