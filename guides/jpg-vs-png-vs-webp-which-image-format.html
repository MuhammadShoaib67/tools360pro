JPG vs PNG vs WebP: which image format should you use?

By Muhammad Shoaib · Published 19 November 2026 · Updated 26 November 2026

Every image on your phone or on a website is stored in a format, and the format decides two things: how big the file is and what it can do. JPG keeps photographs small, PNG keeps edges sharp and backgrounds transparent, and WebP does a little of both. Here is how to choose between them — and how to switch from one to another in seconds.

The short answer

If it is a photograph, use JPG — or WebP if it is going on a website. If it needs a transparent background, use PNG or WebP. If it is a logo, a screenshot or a graphic with text, use PNG. If loading speed matters, use WebP. That covers most cases. The rest of this guide explains why each format fits its job, so the choice stops feeling like a guess — and shows how to convert between them with our free Convert Image tool, which runs entirely in your browser.

JPG: the format for photographs

Lossy compression: small files, lost detail

JPG is the oldest of the three and still the default for photographs. It uses lossy compression: it discards fine detail the eye barely notices — a little texture in a sky, a little noise in a shadow — and in exchange it shrinks a photo to a fraction of its original size. A phone photograph that would be 10 MB or more as a PNG is often 2–3 MB as a JPG, with no visible difference at normal viewing size.

Where JPG works — and its limits

That is why JPG suits photographs, email attachments and uploads to forms and social media: small files, universal support, good-enough quality. Its limits are just as clear. JPG has no transparency, so a clear background becomes white, and every time a JPG is re-saved at a lower quality it loses a little more detail. It is a format for sharing finished photos, not for editing them again and again.

PNG: the format for sharp edges

PNG is lossless: it stores every pixel exactly as it is, with no detail discarded. That makes it the right choice for anything with sharp edges — logos, screenshots, diagrams, graphics with text — where JPG's compression would smear the lines. PNG also supports full transparency, so a logo can sit on any background without a white box around it.

The price is file size. A photograph saved as PNG can be three or four times larger than the same photograph as JPG, because PNG refuses to throw anything away. Converting a JPG to PNG cannot add quality back, either: it only stores the same pixels in a bigger file. Use PNG when exactness matters — a logo, a screenshot, a graphic — and JPG when the picture is a photograph and size matters.

WebP: the modern all-rounder

Nearly JPG-sized, with transparency

WebP is Google's answer to both: it compresses photographs nearly as well as JPG and keeps transparency like PNG, and it can do both losslessly or with lossy compression. In practice a WebP is often 25–34% smaller than the same image as JPG at similar quality, which is why websites that switch to WebP load noticeably faster.

Support in modern browsers — and older software

Support is no longer a worry for the web: WebP works in all modern browsers, including Chrome, Firefox, Safari and Edge. The remaining catch is older software — some apps, printers and upload forms still refuse WebP files. When one does, convert the file back to JPG or PNG; it takes seconds, and the original is never changed.

How to choose in ten seconds

  • Is it a photograph you will share or email? JPG.
  • Does it need a transparent background? PNG or WebP.
  • Is it a logo, screenshot or graphic with text? PNG.
  • Is it for a website where loading speed matters? WebP.
  • Does a form or app accept only one format? Convert to the one it asks for.
  • When two answers fit, pick the smaller file — usually WebP for the web and JPG everywhere else.

What converting can and cannot do

Converting changes how the pixels are stored, not how many there are. A 4,000-pixel-wide photo converted from PNG to JPG is still 4,000 pixels wide — only the file is smaller. If the picture itself is too big for its purpose, resize it after converting; converting alone never changes dimensions.

It also cannot add detail that is not there. A small, blurry image stays small and blurry in every format, and a JPG turned into a PNG does not gain back the detail its compression removed — it just gets bigger. And remember that JPG has no transparency: converting a transparent PNG to JPG fills the clear areas with white, so check the result before you send it.

File size in practice

A typical phone photograph shows the gap clearly. At full size it might be about 2–3 MB as a JPG, 10 MB or more as a PNG, and roughly 1.5–2 MB as a WebP — the same picture, three very different files. That is why the format matters most when files are shared, uploaded or loaded on a web page: the difference between a 10 MB image and a 2 MB one is the difference between a page that loads and a page that stalls.

For the web, the habit that pays off is simple: export photographs as WebP or JPG at a quality around 80–90%, keep logos and graphics as PNG, and convert the odd file that a form refuses rather than re-shooting it.

Questions people ask

Which format is best for photos?

JPG for sharing and email, WebP for websites. Both keep photographs small with good quality; WebP is usually the smaller of the two. PNG is the wrong choice for photos because it stores every pixel exactly and the file ends up several times larger.

Is WebP better than JPG?

For websites, usually yes: WebP gives similar quality at a smaller file size, and it supports transparency, which JPG cannot. For sharing anywhere else, JPG is the safer choice because every app and device accepts it, while some older software still refuses WebP.

Does converting JPG to PNG improve quality?

No. PNG is lossless, but it can only store the pixels it is given — it cannot restore detail that JPG compression already removed. The PNG will be larger than the JPG, not better. Convert JPG to PNG only when you need transparency or perfectly sharp edges.

Which format should I use for a website logo?

PNG if the logo needs a transparent background, or WebP if the site is built for modern browsers and speed matters. Keep the original logo file safe either way, and export the web version from it rather than converting a small JPG back up.
