How to convert PDF to Word on iPhone (free)

By Muhammad Shoaib · Published 21 November 2026 · Updated 21 November 2026

Your iPhone opens PDFs beautifully — in the Files app, in Mail, in Safari — but opening is not converting. iOS has no built-in way to turn a PDF into an editable Word document, so the file that arrives as a fixed page has to be converted somewhere else: in your browser, in the Microsoft Word app, or in Google Docs. Here are the three free ways that actually work on an iPhone, and what to do when the PDF is a scan.

What your iPhone can and cannot do

The Files app is a good PDF viewer: it opens the file, lets you search inside it, and offers markup tools for signing and highlighting. What it cannot do is save that PDF as a Word file — there is no Export to Word option anywhere in iOS, and the same is true of the Books app. So the conversion has to happen in an app that understands Word documents, or in a browser tool that builds one.

Before you start, check which kind of PDF you have, because it decides which methods will work at all. Open the PDF in the Files app, long-press a sentence, and try to select and copy it. If you can select and copy real text, it is a text PDF — a report, a contract, a form exported from Word or Google Docs — and every method below will work. If the whole page selects as one big image, or nothing selects at all, it is a scan: a photograph of printed pages. No converter can extract text that is not there, so skip ahead to the OCR section first.

Option 1: convert in Safari (fastest, nothing to install)

If you need the document now, the browser method is the quickest: no app to install, no account, no payment, and it works on any iPhone — including a borrowed phone where you cannot install anything.

  1. Open the PDF to Word tool in Safari. It opens straight in the browser.
  2. Tap the box and choose your PDF. Safari opens the Files picker, so you can pick the file from On My iPhone, iCloud Drive, Downloads, or Google Drive. Text-based PDFs convert directly: the tool reads the text page by page and rebuilds it as a genuine .docx file.
  3. Download the .docx. Safari saves it to Files, in Downloads. Tap it to open it in Word, Pages, or the Google Docs app — or long-press it and choose Share to send it wherever it needs to go.
  4. Do the five-minute tidy-up below before you start writing — fixing structure first is much faster than fixing it around new edits.

Because the conversion runs in your browser, the document never leaves your iPhone — which is the point of this method for contracts, CVs, and anything personal. Most converters upload your file to a server; this one does not. The honest trade is the same as on a computer: you get clean, editable text, but the original layout, columns, tables, and images are not kept. Option 2: the Microsoft Word app (best formatting)

If Word is already on your iPhone, this is the most convenient method — and because .docx is Word's own format, it usually keeps more of the original formatting than any other free option. The app is free to download, and opening a PDF and converting it works with a free Microsoft account; a Microsoft 365 subscription is not required for the conversion itself.

  1. Find the PDF in the Files app and long-press it, then tap Share — or open it and tap the Share icon.
  2. Tap Open in Word. Word shows a message saying it will convert the PDF into an editable Word document; tap OK or Convert to continue.
  3. Wait a few seconds while Word rebuilds the document, then check the result. Headings, paragraphs, and bold text usually survive; complex tables, columns, and headers may need a small fix.
  4. Save the file. Tap the three-dot menu and save the document as .docx — to On My iPhone, iCloud Drive, or OneDrive, whichever you use.

Word is the best choice when the document is ordinary — a report, an essay, a letter — and you already use Word on your phone. It is a poor choice for a scanned PDF: Word may simply embed the scanned page as a picture instead of extracting its text, leaving you with a Word file you still cannot edit.

Option 3: Google Docs (free, good with plain text)

Google Docs converts PDFs surprisingly well for plain text, and it costs nothing beyond a Google account. The price is privacy: the file is uploaded to Google, so use this only for documents you are comfortable storing there.

  1. Upload the PDF to Google Drive — from the Files app, long-press the PDF, tap Share, and choose Drive; or open the Drive app and tap the plus button to upload it.
  2. Open the PDF in Drive and tap Open with Google Docs. Google converts the file into a Docs document, with the text editable and the images placed roughly where they were.
  3. Download it as Word. In the Docs app, tap the three-dot menu, then Share & export, then Save as Word (.docx). The file lands in your Drive, ready to download or share.

Docs keeps simple layouts tidier than a text-only converter, but it still rearranges multi-column pages and fancy tables, and it drops some fonts. Treat the result as a draft: open it, compare the first page against the original PDF, and fix the structure before you start editing.

Scanned PDFs need OCR first

A scanned PDF is a picture of pages, not a document with text inside — and every method above will either fail or hand you a Word file full of images. The missing step is OCR: software that reads the words out of the picture and turns them into real, editable characters.

On an iPhone you can do this without installing anything. First turn the PDF pages into images with our PDF to Image tool, which runs in Safari. Then read the text from each image with our Image to Text (OCR) tool, copy the recognised text, and paste it into a new Word document in the Word, Pages, or Google Docs app. It takes a few minutes for a long document, and you should proofread the result — OCR misreads the occasional word, especially in handwriting or faint print — but it is the only route that turns a scan into text you can actually edit. If the scan is a single page, the same two tools handle it in under a minute.

The five-minute tidy-up after conversion

No free converter reproduces a PDF perfectly, so expect a short clean-up pass before the document is ready to send. It is the same pass whatever method you used, and doing it before you start writing saves untangling your edits later.

  • Fix the headings first. A converter may leave headings as large bold text instead of real heading styles. Select each heading and apply Heading 1, Heading 2, and so on — the document's structure, and any table of contents, depends on it.
  • Repair broken tables. Tables are the first thing to fall apart: a converter may turn a table into plain paragraphs, or merge cells that were separate. Rebuild any table that matters rather than patching a broken one line by line.
  • Check page breaks and spacing. Extra blank pages and doubled line spacing are common after conversion. Delete the stray breaks, set the line spacing back to single or 1.15, and check the margins match the original.
  • Re-add the images. Text-only converters do not carry photos, logos, or diagrams across. If the document needs them, insert the images from the original PDF yourself — a screenshot of the page, cropped tight, is often the fastest way on a phone.
  • Proofread the first and last pages. Conversion errors cluster at the start and the end of a document — headers, footers, and page numbers land in odd places. A two-minute read of those pages catches most of them.

Which method should you choose?

Pick the Safari browser tool when the document is private or you need it now — nothing is installed and nothing is uploaded. Pick the Word app when Word is already on your iPhone and the document is ordinary — it keeps the most formatting of the free options, but it asks for a Microsoft account. Pick Google Docs when you have no Word and the document is not confidential — it is free and surprisingly good with plain text. And if the PDF is a scan, start with OCR, whichever route you choose afterwards.

One last rule, whichever method you pick: work on a copy, and keep the original PDF. The PDF is your reference for what the document should look like — never convert the only copy of anything. Questions people ask

Can the Files app convert a PDF to Word on iPhone?

No. The Files app opens and previews PDFs, and lets you mark them up, but it has no way to save a PDF as an editable Word document. You need a converter: a browser tool, the Microsoft Word app, or Google Docs.

What is the free way to convert PDF to Word on iPhone?

The fastest free way is in Safari with our PDF to Word tool: open the tool, pick the PDF from the Files app, and download the .docx. Nothing is installed and the file is never uploaded. The Microsoft Word app is also free for this — it opens a PDF and converts it to an editable document — and Google Docs converts PDFs for free with a Google account, though the file is uploaded to Google.

Do I need a Microsoft 365 subscription to convert a PDF in the Word app?

No. The Word app is free to download and its basic features, including opening a PDF and converting it to an editable Word document, work with a free Microsoft account. A Microsoft 365 subscription is not required for the conversion itself.

Can I convert a scanned PDF to Word on iPhone?

Not directly: a scanned PDF is a picture of pages, and it contains no text for a converter to extract. You need OCR first — turn the PDF pages into images with our PDF to Image tool, read the text with our Image to Text (OCR) tool, then paste the text into a new Word document. Word for iOS may also leave a scanned page as an image instead of extracting its text.
