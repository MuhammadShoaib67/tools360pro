How to convert PNG to JPG

PNG is the quality-first format: lossless, sharp, and honest about every pixel — and often five to ten times larger than the same picture saved as a JPG. That size is the whole reason to convert: email limits, upload caps, marketplace listings and web pages all punish heavy files. Here is how to convert PNG to JPG, what you give up in exchange, and how to keep the quality you actually need.

Why JPG files are so much smaller

PNG is a lossless format: it stores every pixel exactly as it is, which is wonderful for quality and terrible for file size. JPG is a lossy format: it looks at the picture, works out which tiny colour changes the human eye barely notices, and throws that information away. A photograph has thousands of near-identical shades, so JPG can discard a large share of the data and still produce a picture that looks the same at normal viewing size. That single difference is why a PNG screenshot that weighs 2 MB often becomes a JPG of a few hundred kilobytes — the same picture, a fraction of the weight.

The saving depends on what the picture contains. Photographs compress dramatically because their colours blend smoothly. Screenshots with flat colours, sharp edges and small text compress less well — JPG was designed for photos, not for crisp lines — but they still shrink to a fraction of the PNG. Either way, the converted file travels faster, uploads where the PNG was refused, and costs less storage everywhere it lands.

What you lose: transparency and a little quality

JPG cannot store transparency, so any see-through areas in your PNG are placed on a white background during conversion. For photos and ordinary screenshots this makes no difference at all, because they have no transparent pixels to begin with. For logos, icons and graphics with see-through backgrounds, the white fill changes how the image looks when placed on a coloured page — the logo will sit inside a white box instead of blending in. If transparency matters, convert to WebP instead, which keeps it, or keep the PNG.

The other loss is quality, and it is small but real. At a high quality setting (around 90% and above) the difference from the original is invisible in normal viewing — you would have to zoom in and compare pixels to find it. The damage comes from converting again and again: every JPG save discards a little more, so a file converted from a JPG, then converted again, slowly goes soft. Always convert from the original PNG, never from an already-converted JPG, and keep the PNG as your high-quality master.

How to convert PNG to JPG

  1. In your browser — fastest, nothing to install. Use the PNG to JPG Converter: drop one or more PNG images onto the box, set the quality (92% is a good default for photos and screenshots), press Convert, and download each JPG or grab them all as a ZIP. The conversion runs on your own device, so the pictures are never uploaded anywhere.
  2. On Windows, with Paint. Open the PNG in Paint, choose File, then Save as, then JPEG picture. It works on every version of Windows and needs no download — but it converts one file at a time and gives you no control over the quality setting, so it is best for the occasional single image.
  3. On a Mac, with Preview. Open the PNG in Preview, choose File, then Export, pick JPEG as the format, and drag the quality slider to where you want it. Preview also converts one file at a time, but the slider gives you the same quality control as the browser tool.
  4. Check the result before you send it. Open the converted JPG and zoom to 100%, especially on small text or fine detail. If it looks soft, convert again from the original PNG at a higher quality setting rather than converting the already-converted JPG — converting a JPG a second time only loses more.

When to keep the PNG

Do not convert when the picture still has work to do. If you plan to edit it further — cropping, retouching, adding text — keep the PNG as the working file, because every JPG save in the editing chain costs a little quality. The same applies to line art, diagrams and screenshots where razor-sharp text matters: JPG softens hard edges slightly, and the artefacts show as faint smudges around letters on flat backgrounds.

And if the image relies on a transparent background — a logo, an icon, a product cutout — PNG to JPG is simply the wrong conversion. The white box it creates is permanent. For those images, PNG to WebP is the better move: much smaller than PNG, transparency intact, and supported by every modern browser.

Questions people ask

Does converting PNG to JPG reduce quality?

Yes, slightly — JPG is a lossy format, so a little image data is discarded. At a quality setting of 90% or above the difference is invisible in normal viewing. The real risk is converting the same picture repeatedly: always convert from the original PNG, and keep the PNG as your master copy.

Why is my JPG on a white background?

JPG cannot store transparency. Any transparent pixels in the PNG are placed on a white background when the JPG is created. If the white box ruins a logo or icon, convert to WebP instead — it keeps transparency and still produces a much smaller file than PNG.

What quality setting should I use?

92% is a good default for most pictures. Smooth photos look fine even at 75-85%. Screenshots with small text deserve 95% or higher, because JPG softens sharp edges first. If the file is for a form with a strict size limit, lower the quality until it fits — but check the result at full size before submitting.
