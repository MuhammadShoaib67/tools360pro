How to Flip a Coin Online

By Muhammad Shoaib · Published 17 November 2026 · Updated 17 November 2026

Heads or tails? When there is no coin in your pocket, a virtual coin flip settles it in a second — fairly, visibly, and with a running count so you can watch the odds play out. This guide shows how it works, why it is fair, and when a coin flip is the right way to decide.

How a virtual coin flip works

Each flip uses your browser's cryptographic random number generator to pick heads or tails, with each side having an exactly equal chance. The coin animation is just for show — the result is decided the moment you click, and the spin plays out over about three quarters of a second before the result is revealed.

The running count tracks every flip in the session: how many heads, how many tails, and the heads share as a percentage. The count lives only on this page and resets when you reload it, so it is a tally for one session of flipping, not a permanent record.

Is a virtual coin flip fair?

A virtual coin is fairer than a real one, in a small way. A real coin can be biased by its weight distribution, the way it is flipped, and the surface it lands on — and studies have found that a coin flipped by hand lands on the side it started on slightly more often than half the time. A digital flip has no starting side and no physics: it is a fresh random choice every time.

That does not mean the results look even. Ten flips can easily come out 7-3 or even 9-1 — that is normal randomness, not bias. The 50-50 split only emerges over many flips: after a hundred flips, the count is usually within a few of even, and after a thousand, closer still. Short runs are streaky by nature.

Flip a coin online in four steps

  1. Open the flipper. Go to the Coin Flip page — no sign-up, no install, nothing to set up.
  2. Click Flip the coin. The coin spins and lands on heads or tails. The result appears under the coin.
  3. Read the running count. Heads, tails, total flips and the heads share are tracked below, so you can follow a best-of series or watch the split even out.
  4. Flip again or reset. Flip as many times as you like, or click Reset count to start a fresh tally.

When a coin flip is the right way to decide

A coin flip is the right tool when two options are genuinely close and the cost of deciding exceeds the cost of either outcome: who goes first, which restaurant to try, whether to watch one film or another. It settles the question in a second and both sides can accept the result as fair, because neither chose it.

It is also a surprisingly good thinking tool. Flip the coin, and before it lands, notice which side you are hoping for — that hope is your real preference, and it often matters more than the result itself. If the flip disappoints you, you have learned something: you already knew what you wanted. For anything with real stakes, though, decide on the merits — a coin flip is for settling ties, not for making important choices for you.

Other ways to settle a two-way choice

A coin flip is the fastest settler, but it is not the only one. A wheel spinner suits choices with more than two options. Drawing straws works when the choosers are in the same room. Rock, paper, scissors adds a layer of psychology — and a layer of argument about what counts as a throw.

For a remote decision between two people, a shared coin flip page is the simplest option: one person flips, both see the result, and the count is there if anyone questions it. For anything that needs a record, screenshot the result — the count resets when the page reloads.

Questions people ask

Is a virtual coin flip really 50-50?

Yes. Each flip uses your browser's cryptographic random number generator, giving heads and tails an exactly equal chance. Short runs can still look lopsided — that is normal randomness.

Does the count reset if I reload the page?

Yes. The heads and tails tally lives only on this page for the current session. Reloading starts a fresh count, and nothing is stored anywhere.

Can I flip best-of-three or best-of-five?

Flip as many times as you like and read the running count — the tally makes it easy to track a best-of series yourself.

Why does the coin sometimes land on the same side several times?

Because each flip is independent. A run of heads does not make tails more likely next time — the odds are 50-50 on every single flip, no matter what came before.
