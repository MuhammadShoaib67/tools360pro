How to Compress a Video

Compressing a video means making the same video take up less space: the same length, the same picture, a smaller file. This guide shows you how to do it free in your browser with the Video Compressor — no sign-up, no app to install, and your file never leaves your device.

What compressing a video means

Every video file is a stream of pictures with a soundtrack, squeezed by a codec into as few bytes as the encoder thinks it can get away with. The number that decides the file size is the bitrate: how many bits of data the video uses per second. A phone recording at 20 megabits per second is a big file; the same recording re-encoded at 2.5 megabits per second is roughly eight times smaller.

Compressing a video means re-encoding it at a lower bitrate, and sometimes at a smaller resolution too. Nothing about the length changes, and the sound carries over — the picture just carries a little less detail. That is the whole trade: size against quality. A video recorded at a high bitrate has detail to spare, so it shrinks a lot before the difference is visible. A video that is already heavily compressed has little left to give, and squeezing it further mostly shows as blocky patches in fast-moving scenes.

How to compress a video in four steps

  1. Open the Video Compressor and drop in your file. Open the Video Compressor and drag an MP4, WebM or MOV file onto the dropzone, or click it to browse. The file is decoded in your browser — it is never uploaded anywhere.
  2. Pick a quality preset. High is about 5 Mbps at 1080p, Medium about 2.5 Mbps and Low about 1 Mbps, with the bitrate scaled down for smaller resolutions. Medium is the everyday choice; High keeps the most detail, Low gives the smallest file.
  3. Pick a resolution, then press Compress Video. Keep the original resolution when the video will be watched on a big screen; pick 720p or 480p when it will only be watched on a phone or in a chat. Compression takes about as long as the video itself, because the video is re-encoded in real time, and the progress bar shows exactly where it is.
  4. Check the sizes, then download. The tool shows the original size, the compressed size and the percentage saved. Preview the compressed video, and if it looks right, click Download Compressed WebM — the file downloads with “-compressed” added to its name.

Choosing a quality preset and resolution

Start with Medium at the original resolution. It suits most jobs: a phone recording, a screen capture, a clip for a chat or an upload form. The file shrinks a lot and the picture still looks clean on a phone or laptop screen, because phone cameras and screen recorders usually record at a much higher bitrate than the screen can show.

Resolution is the bigger lever. Dropping from 1080p to 720p cuts the number of pixels by more than half, so the same preset produces a much smaller file — and on a phone screen the difference is hard to spot. Dropping to 480p shrinks it further, for videos that will only ever be watched small. Two things to know: the tool never upscales, so picking a resolution larger than your video simply keeps the original; and picking a smaller resolution caps the frame rate at up to 30 frames per second, because each frame is drawn onto a canvas at the new size before it is recorded.

Which formats work

The compressor accepts any format your browser can play and decode: MP4 (H.264), WebM and MOV in most browsers. That is because the decoding is done by the browser itself, using the same video decoder that plays videos on every website. The compressor never sees a file format it cannot decode — it hands the file to the browser and works with the frames that come back.

If a file will not load, the browser cannot decode that format or codec, and the compressor will say so plainly instead of failing silently. The fix is usually to try an MP4 file, which every browser plays, or to open the tool in another browser, which may have a different decoder. Files from phones and cameras are usually MP4, and both work in every modern browser.

Why the download is a WebM file

Browsers can decode many video formats, but the recorder built into the browser writes WebM — there is no MP4 recorder in Chrome, Edge or Firefox, and Safari only gained WebM recording in version 18.4. A compressor that promised an MP4 file would have to ship a large encoder library or send your file to a server to be encoded there, and neither fits a tool that promises your files never leave your device. So the compressor downloads WebM: the format the browser can honestly produce.

WebM plays in Chrome, Edge, Firefox and VLC, and in most modern video players and editors. If the place you are sending the video to only accepts MP4, convert the WebM afterwards in any video app: open it, export or save as MP4, and the compressed video is ready to share anywhere. The size saving survives the conversion, because the video is already compressed by then.

What to do with a compressed video

The most common use is sharing: a smaller file attaches to emails, passes upload-form size limits, and sends in a chat without a long wait. A compressed video also stores better — a phone full of recordings takes up far less space once the clips you want to keep are re-encoded at a sensible bitrate. And because the original file is never touched, you can always come back and compress it again at a different preset if the first result is not what you wanted.

Compression pairs well with the other tools on this site. If the audio is the part you want, pull it out first with the MP4 to MP3 Converter and skip the video entirely. If you want the words as well as the pictures, run the video through the AI Audio Transcriber to get a transcript. Each tool does one job, and the jobs chain together.

The honest limits

A browser compressor has limits worth knowing. It compresses one file at a time — there is no batch mode, and no way to trim or cut a video. Compression takes about as long as the video itself, because the video is re-encoded in real time, so a long video needs patience. And the download is always a WebM, never an MP4, for the reason above: browsers record WebM.

There is also no undo after a download — but that is not a real limit, because the original file is never changed. Compression here reads your file and writes a new one; the original stays exactly as it was, on your device, ready to be compressed again differently. If a result comes out too soft, open the original, pick a higher preset, and download again.

Questions people ask

What video formats can I compress online?

Any format your browser can play and decode: MP4 (H.264), WebM and MOV in most browsers. If a file will not load, the browser cannot decode that format or codec — try an MP4 file, or open the tool in another browser.

Why does the compressor download a WebM file?

Browsers record WebM, not MP4 — there is no MP4 recorder in Chrome, Edge or Firefox. The compressor downloads a WebM file, which plays in Chrome, Edge, Firefox and VLC. If you need an MP4, convert the WebM afterwards in any video app.

How much smaller will my video get?

It depends on the original: a phone recording at a high bitrate shrinks a lot, while a video that is already heavily compressed shrinks little and can even grow slightly at the High preset. The compressor shows the original and compressed sizes before you download, so you see the exact saving.

Is my video uploaded when I compress it online?

Not with this compressor. Your file is decoded, re-encoded and downloaded entirely in your browser — it never leaves your device, and nothing about it is stored or sent anywhere.
