How to convert PNG to WebP

WebP is the format PNG users have been waiting for: it keeps the transparency PNG is loved for and cuts the file size to a fraction of it. The catch is compatibility — WebP works in every modern browser, but some older software, forms and apps still refuse it. Here is how to convert PNG to WebP, how much smaller the files get, and the check worth doing before you commit.

What WebP does better than PNG

WebP is Google's image format, built to do the jobs of PNG and JPG in a single file. Like PNG, it supports transparency — a logo with a see-through background stays see-through after conversion. Like JPG, it can compress lossily, discarding data the eye barely notices. And it also has a lossless mode, which Google reports produces files about a quarter smaller than the same image saved as PNG. In practice, a PNG screenshot or graphic often becomes a WebP half its size or less, with no visible change at all.

That combination is why websites have adopted WebP so widely: pages load faster, use less bandwidth, and look exactly the same. For a site owner converting a library of PNG graphics, the saving multiplies across every image on every page. For one person sending a picture, the difference is a file that fits the upload limit instead of being refused by it.

The one problem: compatibility

WebP works in every modern browser — Chrome, Edge, Firefox and Safari have all supported it since 2020 — so anything that stays on the web is safe. The trouble starts outside the browser: some older desktop programs, upload forms, email clients and older phones still do not recognise a .webp file. A form that asks for a PNG or JPG may reject a WebP outright, and an old version of a design tool may not open one.

The fix is simple: check the destination before you convert. If the image is for a website, a modern app or your own storage, WebP is a good choice. If it is for a form, a printer or an unknown recipient, confirm that WebP is accepted — or keep a PNG copy alongside it. Your originals are never touched by the conversion, so keeping both costs nothing but a little disk space.

How to convert PNG to WebP

  1. In your browser — fastest, nothing to install. Use the PNG to WebP Converter: drop one or more PNG images onto the box, set the quality, press Convert, and download each WebP or grab them all as a ZIP. The conversion runs on your own device, so the pictures are never uploaded anywhere.
  2. With GIMP, the free desktop editor. Open the PNG in GIMP, choose File, then Export As, and change the filename to end in .webp. GIMP shows a quality slider and a lossless option, so you can choose exactly how the file is compressed. It is the best choice when you are already editing the picture.
  3. With Squoosh, Google's free web app. Open squoosh.app, drop the image in, and choose WebP on the right-hand side. Squoosh shows the original and the converted version side by side with a slider, so you can see exactly what the compression does before you download. It is a good way to learn what different quality settings look like.
  4. Check transparency and acceptance. Open the converted WebP and view it against a coloured background to confirm the transparent areas are still transparent. Then check that the place you are sending it to accepts WebP — if it does not, the PNG original is still there, untouched.

When to keep the PNG

Keep the PNG when the destination demands it, and keep it as your archive master either way. PNG has thirty years of universal support behind it; WebP has about fifteen. For a file you may need to open in ten years on unknown software, PNG is the safer long-term bet. The same applies to pictures you plan to edit heavily — a lossless PNG is the safest working file, and you can always export a WebP from it later.

For everyday use, though, WebP is the better working format: smaller files, same transparency, same quality at a glance. The sensible habit is to keep the PNG as the master and hand out WebP copies wherever they are accepted.

Questions people ask

Does WebP keep transparency?

Yes. WebP supports an alpha channel in both its lossy and lossless modes, so a PNG with a transparent background converts to a WebP with a transparent background. View the result against a coloured background to confirm — if the background shows through, transparency survived.

Is WebP really smaller than PNG?

Yes, usually by a lot. Google reports lossless WebP at about a quarter smaller than PNG, and lossy WebP can be half the size or less with no visible difference. The exact saving depends on the picture — flat graphics and screenshots shrink the most.

Why won't an app accept my WebP file?

Some older software, upload forms and devices were built before WebP existed and never learned to read it. If a destination rejects WebP, convert the same picture to PNG or JPG instead — the original PNG is still there, and both formats are accepted essentially everywhere.
