How to add a drop shadow to an image

By Muhammad Shoaib · Published 17 November 2026 · Updated 17 November 2026

A drop shadow is the difference between a flat cut-out and an object that seems to float. Here is how to add one that looks real — why shadows follow shape, the three settings that matter, and four ways to do it, starting with the fastest.

What makes a shadow look real

Real shadows are soft, grey and offset in the direction light does not come from. Sunlight and ceiling lights usually fall from above, so our eyes expect a shadow slightly below and to one side of the object. A shadow that matches that expectation reads as depth; one that does not — a shadow above the object, or a perfectly centred halo — reads as a mistake, even if the viewer cannot say why.

Softness matters as much as direction. A hard-edged shadow looks like a second copy of the object, not a shadow. Real shadows blur with distance: the further the object floats from the surface, the softer its shadow. A blur of 15 to 30 pixels gives the soft edge of a genuine shadow; opacity of 30% to 50% gives the grey of one. The most common beginner error is a shadow that is too dark, too sharp and too large — when in doubt, make it softer and lighter than you think.

Why cut-outs shadow best

A drop shadow follows the opaque shape of the image. On a logo with a transparent background, the shadow hugs the letters or the icon — exactly the floating effect people want. On a full rectangular photo, there is no shape to follow, so the shadow appears only around the edges: a subtle lift, but not the dramatic float of a cut-out.

That is why the usual workflow has two steps: remove the background first, then add the shadow. A product photo with its background removed and a soft shadow added looks like a professional studio shot — the product floats on white, grounded by its shadow. Do it in that order, and keep the original photo: a shadow baked into a file cannot be removed, so always work on a copy.

Shadows in design: less is more

Shadows are seasoning, not the meal. One soft shadow on a logo or a product photo adds depth; shadows on every element of a page add noise. The designs people call “clean” usually have exactly one shadow style used consistently — the same offset, blur and opacity everywhere — which is what makes the page feel coherent.

Consistency also means direction. If one image's shadow falls down and right while another's falls down and left, the light source seems to move between pictures, and the page feels subtly wrong. Pick one direction — down and slightly right is the safe default — and use it for every shadow on the page.

How to add a drop shadow to an image

  1. In your browser — fastest, nothing to install. Use the Image Shadow Maker: drop the image in, set the offset, blur, colour and opacity, and download as a transparent PNG. The tool adds padding so the shadow is never cut off.
  2. In Canva. Select the image, click Effects, then Shadows, and choose a preset — or adjust the offset, blur and transparency sliders. Canva's shadows are live effects, so you can change them any time before downloading.
  3. In GIMP. Open the image, choose Filters, then Light and Shadow, then Drop Shadow. Set the offset, blur radius and opacity, and GIMP draws the shadow on a new layer — which you can adjust or delete afterwards. GIMP is free.
  4. In PowerPoint or Google Slides. Select the picture, open the Format options, and turn on Shadow. You can set the distance, blur and transparency. This suits slides and documents where the shadow only needs to exist in the presentation, not in the image file.

When not to add a shadow

Not every image wants a shadow. Small icons at 16 or 32 pixels turn to mush with a shadow — there are not enough pixels for a soft edge, so the icon just looks dirty. Text-heavy graphics and diagrams do not need depth; a shadow behind a chart adds nothing and can reduce readability. And photographs of real scenes already contain their own shadows — adding a drawn one on top looks artificial.

The test is simple: does the shadow help the viewer understand the image, or does it decorate it? A shadow under a product photo says “this is an object you could pick up” — it helps. A shadow under a screenshot says nothing. When the answer is nothing, skip it.

Questions people ask

Why does my photo barely show a shadow?

The shadow follows the image's opaque shape, and a full photo is an opaque rectangle — so the shadow only appears around its edges. Shadows show best on logos and cut-out images with transparent backgrounds.

Should I download as PNG or JPG?

PNG, to keep the transparent padding the shadow needs. JPG cannot be transparent, so the padding becomes white and the shadow sits on a white background.

What settings look most natural?

A small offset down and right (around 10–15 pixels), a blur of 15–30 pixels, and a dark shadow at 30–50% opacity. Real shadows are soft and grey, not hard and black.
