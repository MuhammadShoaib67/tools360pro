How to Use a World Clock Online

By Muhammad Shoaib · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

A world clock answers one question at a glance: what time is it there, right now? One clock for each place you care about — the office in another city, family overseas, a client across the ocean — all ticking together, so you never have to do the maths in your head before a call.

What a world clock shows

Each clock shows the live time in its city, ticking every second, together with the date and the UTC offset — the gap between that city's time and Coordinated Universal Time. Eight clocks load by default: New York, London, Dubai, Karachi, Singapore, Tokyo, Sydney and UTC itself. They cover the Americas, Europe, the Middle East, South Asia, East Asia and Australia, so the places most people call are already on the screen.

The date matters as much as the time. When it is Monday morning in Tokyo it is still Sunday evening in New York — 7pm on a winter Sunday, 8pm in summer — so the two clocks show different dates as well as different times. A world clock that showed only the time would leave you guessing which day it is over there; the date line under each clock removes the guess.

Set up your world clock in three steps

  1. Open the World Clock. Open the World Clock — eight clocks are already ticking: New York, London, Dubai, Karachi, Singapore, Tokyo, Sydney and UTC, with the time, date and UTC offset under each one.
  2. Add the cities you need. Pick a time zone from the dropdown and click Add Clock. The list covers 29 common zones, from Honolulu to Auckland, so most cities you call are one click away.
  3. Remove what you do not need. Click the × in a clock's top-right corner to remove it, or Reset to Defaults to bring the original eight back. Use the Time format dropdown to switch between 24-hour time and 12-hour time with AM/PM.

Which cities to add

Add the places you actually call. A colleague in another city, a client across the ocean, family overseas — one clock per regular contact beats a wall of cities you never ring. A clock earns its place when it answers a question you ask more than once a week; the rest is decoration, and decoration is the first thing to remove when the screen gets crowded.

UTC deserves a clock of its own if you work with anything scheduled in it: servers, flight times, release schedules, and anything where “3pm UTC” is the agreed reference. It is already one of the eight defaults, and it is the one clock that never moves for daylight saving — which makes it the fixed point every other clock can be compared against.

Reading the date as well as the time

The day starts at different moments around the world, so two clocks can show different dates at the same instant. Tokyo is 13 or 14 hours ahead of New York, depending on the season, which means Monday morning in Tokyo is still Sunday evening in New York. A call arranged for “Monday 9am” needs a city attached to it, or one side will turn up a day early.

This is also why the date under each clock is worth a glance before you send a message. “Good morning” to a city where it is already evening is a small embarrassment; a deadline agreed for “Friday” when the other side's Friday started fourteen hours before yours is a bigger one. The clock shows the date so you do not have to work it out.

12-hour or 24-hour time

The Time format dropdown switches every clock between 24-hour time and 12-hour time with AM/PM. It is display only — the times are the same either way, just written differently. Twenty-four-hour time avoids AM/PM mix-ups, which is why it suits scheduling: 15:00 can only mean one thing, while 3:00 needs an AM or a PM to be sure. Twelve-hour time is how most people speak, so it suits a quick glance before a call. Pick the one you read fastest, and switch any time.

The honest limits

A browser world clock has limits worth knowing. Your added clocks are not saved: reloading the page brings back the original eight clocks, so set up your cities again after a refresh. The clocks use your device clock, so a wrong device clock means wrong times everywhere — most devices keep their clocks accurate automatically, but one set by hand can drift.

There are no alarms and no meeting scheduler. The clock shows the time in each city; it does not remind you to call, and it does not find a slot that suits everyone. For that, convert the time you want with the Time Zone Converter and send a calendar invite — the world clock tells you what time it is now, and the converter tells you what time a future meeting will be there.

Questions people ask

How do I add a city to the world clock?

Pick a time zone from the dropdown and click Add Clock. The list covers 29 common zones, from Honolulu to Auckland.

Do my clocks stay after I reload the page?

No. Added clocks are not saved, so reloading the page brings back the original eight clocks. Use Reset to Defaults any time to restore them.

Does the world clock handle daylight saving time?

Yes. Each clock uses the rules for its zone on today's date, so a city on daylight saving shows its summer time automatically.

Why do two cities show different dates?

Because the day starts at different moments around the world. When it is Monday morning in Tokyo it is still Sunday evening in New York, so the two clocks show different dates.
