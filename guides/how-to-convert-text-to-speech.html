How to Convert Text to Speech

Converting text to speech in your browser takes ten seconds: paste your text, pick a voice, press Speak, and your device reads it aloud. No sign-up, no app, no upload — the speech is generated on your device, so your text never leaves it. There is one honest limit: the browser plays speech aloud but does not save it as an audio file.

How browser text to speech works

Your browser has a built-in speech engine, and a text-to-speech tool simply hands it your text. The voices come from your device: Windows, macOS, Android and iOS all ship with voices installed, and browsers add their own. That is why the voice list looks different on every device — a phone may offer a handful, a desktop may offer dozens, and the quality varies with the voice.

Because the speech is generated on your device, nothing is uploaded and nothing is recorded. There is no account, no usage limit and no cost: the feature is part of the browser, and the tool is just a friendly way to use it. The trade-off is that you get the voices your device has, not a studio catalogue — but for listening to an article or checking how a sentence sounds, they are more than good enough.

Listening to your own writing

Hearing your words read aloud is one of the best editing tricks there is. Awkward sentences, repeated words and missing words all jump out when you listen, even when your eyes have read the same paragraph ten times and missed them. Writers, students and professionals use text to speech to proofread essays, emails and reports before sending them.

It helps with accessibility too. Long articles become listenable, tired eyes get a rest, and people who absorb information better by ear can listen instead of reading. Adjust the speed to match your comfort — a little faster than normal speech is often easier to follow for long text, while a slower speed suits careful proofreading.

Convert text to speech in four steps

  1. Copy your text. Select the text you want to hear — an article, an essay, an email draft — and copy it. For very long articles, paste one section at a time.
  2. Paste it into the tool. Open the Text to Speech tool and paste your text into the box. Nothing is uploaded; the speech is generated on your device.
  3. Pick a voice and speed. Choose a voice from the list, then set the speed slider — a little faster than normal suits long text, slower suits proofreading — and the pitch if you want a higher or lower voice.
  4. Click Speak. Listen, pause when you need to, and stop when you are done. If a sentence sounds wrong, it probably reads wrong too — fix it and listen again.

What browser text to speech cannot do

The browser plays speech aloud and that is all it does: there is no audio file to download, because the browser speaks the text in real time and does not record it. Saving speech as an MP3 needs a server-side service that generates and stores an audio file, which is a different kind of tool entirely — and one that would need your text sent to a server.

If you need an audio file, a screen recorder can capture the sound while the tool speaks, or a dedicated text-to-speech service can generate one. But for listening — proofreading, learning a pronunciation, resting your eyes — the browser tool does the job privately and without asking you to sign up for anything.

Other ways to hear text read aloud

Most devices have speech built in beyond the browser: screen readers read whole screens aloud, e-book apps read books, and operating systems offer a speak-selection shortcut. Those are better for long daily listening. Word processors can read a document aloud too, which suits proofreading a draft you are already editing.

The browser tool wins when the text is already copied and you want to hear it right now, without opening another app or changing a setting. Paste, press Speak, listen — the whole job takes seconds.

Questions people ask

Can I download the speech as an MP3?

No. This tool plays speech aloud in your browser using your device voices, and the browser does not record it. Saving audio as a file needs a server-side text-to-speech service.

Why do I only see one or two voices?

Voices come from your device and browser, so the list varies. Phones usually offer fewer voices than desktops, and installing extra voices in your system settings adds them to the list.

Does the voice quality depend on my device?

Yes. The speech engine and voices are built into your operating system and browser, so a newer device usually sounds more natural than an older one.

Is there a limit on how much text I can hear?

No fixed limit, but very long texts can be slow to start and some browsers handle long utterances less reliably. For long articles, paste a section at a time.
