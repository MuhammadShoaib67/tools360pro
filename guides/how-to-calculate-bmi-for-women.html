How to calculate BMI for women

By Muhammad Shoaib · Published 23 November 2026 · Updated 26 November 2026

The honest answer first: the formula for women is exactly the same as for men, and so is the chart. There is no separate women's BMI. What makes this guide worth reading is everything around that one number — the worked example, the healthy weight range for your height, and the women-specific context that changes how the result should be read: body fat, pregnancy, and a second measurement that catches what BMI misses. Here is how to calculate BMI for women, step by step, with the limits stated plainly.

The formula — the same for everyone

BMI is your weight in kilograms divided by your height in metres squared. In symbols: BMI = kg ÷ m². If you measure in feet, inches and pounds instead, the equivalent formula is BMI = 703 × pounds ÷ inches², and both give the same answer for the same person. Our BMI Calculator handles both unit systems — choose Metric or Imperial, enter your height and weight, and the result appears live, with the category and the healthy weight range for your height.

A worked example: a woman who is 165 cm tall and weighs 62 kg. First convert the height to metres: 165 cm is 1.65 m. Square it: 1.65 × 1.65 = 2.7225. Then divide the weight by that number: 62 ÷ 2.7225 = 22.8. Her BMI is 22.8, which sits inside the healthy band. The calculation takes ten seconds by hand, and the calculator does it instantly — but knowing the formula matters, because it shows exactly what BMI is: one number built from only two measurements, height and weight, and nothing else. It knows nothing about your muscle, your bone density, where you carry weight, or how active you are. As the CDC notes, BMI does not distinguish between fat, muscle, and bone mass. Keep that in mind for everything that follows.

The chart — also the same for women and men

The adult BMI chart has four bands, and they are the same for women and men: underweight is below 18.5, healthy is 18.5 to 24.9, overweight is 25 to 29.9, and obese is 30 or above. These are the widely used WHO ranges for adults, and our calculator places your number on the chart with a marker so you can see where you sit. If a website offers you a separate "women's BMI chart" with different cut-offs, treat it with suspicion — no major health body publishes sex-specific adult BMI bands, because the formula and the bands were designed to be the same for all adults.

That does not mean the number means the same thing in every body. Women typically carry a higher body-fat percentage than men at the same BMI — roughly ten percentage points higher on average — because women's bodies naturally store more essential fat. So two people with a BMI of 24, one a woman and one a man, can have quite different body compositions. The band is the same; the amount of fat behind it is not. That is the first reason to read BMI as a screening number — a starting point for a conversation — rather than a verdict on its own.

A healthy weight is a range, not a number

Notice how wide each range is — around 14 to 20 kg from one end to the other. That width is the point: a healthy weight is a range, not a single magic number, and where you sit inside it depends on your build, your muscle and your life. If your BMI sits outside the healthy band, the range also shows how far you are from it in kilograms, which is far more concrete than the word overweight on its own. Measure yourself right

A BMI result is only as good as the two numbers that go into it, so measure them carefully. Weigh yourself in the morning, on the same scales, in light clothing — your weight can move by a kilo or more through the day as you eat, drink and move around. Measure your height without shoes, standing straight against a wall, because even a small height error shifts the result: height is squared in the formula, so an error in height counts twice.

If you use feet and inches, convert carefully: five foot five is 65 inches, not 5.5 feet, and that mix-up is an easy way to get a wildly wrong BMI. The calculator takes either unit system, so enter the numbers exactly as you measured them and let it do the conversion. And check again every few weeks rather than every day: weight moves up and down daily, and the trend over a month tells you far more than any single reading.

When BMI does not apply

Pregnancy: BMI does not apply

BMI has real limits, and for women there are three that matter most. First, pregnancy: BMI is not a valid measure during pregnancy at all. Weight gain is expected and needed — the baby, the placenta, extra fluid and increased blood volume all add weight that has nothing to do with body fat. A pre-pregnancy BMI can help a doctor set a healthy weight-gain range, but during pregnancy itself, follow your doctor's guidance rather than a BMI chart.

Muscle and age change what the number means

Second, muscle: BMI cannot tell muscle from fat, so a woman who trains heavily — a weightlifter, a sprinter, a CrossFit athlete — can land in the overweight band while carrying very little body fat. Third, age: after the menopause, muscle is gradually lost and fat is more likely to settle around the waist, so an older woman can have a "healthy" BMI while carrying more body fat than the number suggests. In all three cases the same rule holds: BMI is a screening number, not a diagnosis. If a result worries you, a doctor or dietitian is the right next step, not a search engine.

A second number worth checking: waist circumference

Because BMI cannot say where weight is carried, health bodies recommend a second, simpler measurement alongside it: waist circumference. Fat carried around the waist is more closely linked to health risk than fat carried on the hips and thighs, and a tape measure catches what the BMI formula cannot. Measure around your waist at the level of your belly button, after breathing out, with the tape level and snug but not tight.

For women, the widely used WHO thresholds are 80 cm and 88 cm: a waist of 80 cm or more means increased health risk, and 88 cm or more means substantially increased risk — whatever the BMI says. That makes the pair of numbers far more honest than either alone: a healthy BMI with a waist above 88 cm deserves attention, and a BMI just over 25 with a small waist and an active life is a different picture from the same BMI with a large waist. Questions people ask

How do I calculate BMI for a woman?

The same way as for anyone: divide weight in kilograms by height in metres squared. A woman who is 165 cm tall and weighs 62 kg has a BMI of 62 ÷ 1.65² = 22.8. What is a healthy BMI for a woman?

A BMI between 18.5 and 24.9 is classed as healthy for adult women, on the widely used WHO ranges — the same bands as for men. The number is a screening guide, not a diagnosis: it cannot tell muscle from fat, so read it together with waist circumference and how active you are, and talk to a doctor if a result worries you.

Is the BMI chart different for women?

No — the adult BMI chart is the same for women and men: underweight is below 18.5, healthy is 18.5 to 24.9, overweight is 25 to 29.9, and obese is 30 or above. What differs is interpretation: women typically carry a higher body-fat percentage than men at the same BMI, so the same number can mean a different amount of body fat.
