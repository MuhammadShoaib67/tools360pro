How to convert currency online free

By Muhammad Shoaib · Published 26 November 2026 · Updated 26 November 2026

Converting currency online takes seconds: type an amount, pick two currencies, and the live rate does the rest. But the number you see is not always the number you get — banks, cards and exchange counters all take their cut. This guide shows how to convert currency online free, what the rate actually means, and how to check you are getting a fair deal.

How currency conversion works

An exchange rate is simply the price of one currency in terms of another: how many Pakistani rupees one US dollar buys, or how many euros one pound buys. That price moves all day as currencies are bought and sold on the global market, so a rate from yesterday is already out of date — and a rate from last month can be badly wrong. A good converter therefore fetches a fresh rate for every conversion instead of relying on a stored table.

Our Currency Converter gets its rates live from ExchangeRate-API (open.er-api.com), with the US dollar as the base currency. Conversions between two non-USD currencies are calculated through the dollar: the tool converts your amount to USD first, then to the target currency. That is how most converters work, and it is why the result matches the rate you see quoted anywhere else — every pair is anchored to the same base. The "last updated" time shown under the result is the time reported by the API itself, so you can see how fresh the rate is.

  2. Type the amount. Enter the amount you want to convert in the Amount field — for example, 100 dollars or 50,000 rupees.

The mid-market rate — and why you never get it

The rate a converter shows is the mid-market rate: the fair midpoint between the price at which banks buy a currency and the price at which they sell it. It is the rate quoted on the news and the one currency markets actually trade at. But it is not the rate any bank, card or money changer will give you, because they make their money on the difference — the spread between their buy and sell prices.

That is why the same 100 dollars can turn into noticeably different amounts of rupees depending on where you exchange it. A bank adds its margin, a card network adds its own, and a money changer at an airport adds the largest of all. None of this is hidden exactly — the margin is built into the rate they offer — but it is easy to miss, because nobody advertises "we give you 2% less than the real rate". The honest way to compare is to convert the amount here first, at the mid-market rate, and then see how far each provider's offer sits below it. The smaller the gap, the better the deal.

The fees that hide inside the rate

Card foreign-transaction fees. Many debit and credit cards add a fee — commonly around 1–3% — on purchases made in a foreign currency, on top of the exchange rate itself. A card that advertises "no foreign transaction fee" is genuinely worth having if you shop or travel abroad often, because the saving applies to every single payment.

Dynamic currency conversion. When a shop, hotel or ATM abroad offers to charge you "in your home currency" for convenience, say no and choose the local currency instead. The merchant's conversion rate is almost always worse than your card network's, and the "convenience" can cost several percent on every transaction.

Airport and hotel counters. Exchange counters in airports and hotels offer the worst rates of all, because they know you have no alternative at that moment. If you need cash on arrival, exchange a small amount only, and do the rest at a bank or a reputable exchanger in town — or withdraw from an ATM in local currency and decline its conversion offer.

Transfer services and apps. For sending money across borders, compare the total, not the advertised fee: some services charge no fee but use a poor rate, while others charge a small fee with a rate close to mid-market. Convert the amount here first, then work out what actually lands at the other end — that is the only comparison that matters.

When the rate matters most

Travel. Before a trip, convert your budget at the live rate so you know what a hotel night, a meal or a taxi ride really costs in your own currency — and check again before you exchange cash, because a rate that moved 2% overnight changes a week-long budget by real money.

Online shopping. When a store prices in dollars or euros, convert the total — including shipping — before you pay, and remember the card fee on top. A "bargain" in another currency often stops being one once the conversion and the fee are added.

Freelance invoices and remote work. If you are paid in dollars and live on rupees, the rate on the day the payment arrives decides your actual income. Quoting a client in dollars is fine, but always convert the expected payment at today's rate before you count it as earnings, and keep an eye on the rate if a payment is delayed by a week or more.

For all three, the habit is the same: check the live rate first, compare providers against it, and never accept a conversion offered at the till without knowing what the fair rate is.
