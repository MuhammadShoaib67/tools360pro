How to convert JPG to WebP

WebP does the JPG job better: the same photo, visibly the same quality, in a file roughly a quarter to a third smaller. That is why websites have been switching to WebP for years — faster pages, less bandwidth, same pictures. Here is how to convert JPG to WebP, what changes in the process, and the one compatibility check worth doing first.

How much smaller, really

Google's own tests put lossy WebP at roughly 25-34% smaller than JPEG at the same visual quality, and independent tests have broadly confirmed it. In everyday terms, a 500 KB JPG photo often becomes a WebP of 300-400 KB, and a whole gallery of product photos shrinks by the same proportion. The saving varies with content: smooth photos with gentle gradients compress best, while noisy or highly detailed pictures save less — but WebP beats JPG on size for the great majority of photos.

The saving is real money and real time on a website: smaller images mean faster page loads, lower bandwidth bills, and visitors on slow connections who actually wait for the page. That is the main reason to convert — not because JPG is broken, but because WebP does the same job with less weight.

What changes — and what doesn't

The picture itself barely changes. At a quality setting around 85-90%, a WebP made from a JPG looks the same as the JPG at normal viewing size. One thing to know: the JPG's existing compression artefacts carry over, because the WebP is encoded from the JPG's pixels as they are — converting cannot undo damage that is already there. It also does not add much new damage at high quality settings, so the result is a smaller file that looks like the photo you started with.

What does change is the metadata. Camera settings, location data and other EXIF information stored in the JPG are not carried into the WebP — the conversion keeps the pixels, not the camera data. For most people that is a quiet privacy bonus: a photo shared as WebP does not leak where it was taken. Photographers who need the metadata should keep the original JPG as the archive copy.

How to convert JPG to WebP

  1. In your browser — fastest, nothing to install. Use the JPG to WebP Converter: drop one or more JPG images onto the box, set the quality (85-90% is a good starting point for photos), press Convert, and download each WebP or grab them all as a ZIP. The conversion runs on your own device, so the pictures are never uploaded anywhere.
  2. With Squoosh, Google's free web app. Open squoosh.app, drop the image in, and choose WebP. Squoosh shows the original and the converted version side by side, so you can drag the quality slider and watch exactly what changes before you download. It is the best way to learn what different quality settings do to a photo.
  3. With GIMP, the free desktop editor. Open the JPG in GIMP, choose File, then Export As, and change the filename to end in .webp. GIMP offers a quality slider and an advanced options panel — the best choice when you are already editing the picture or want fine control over the encoding.
  4. Compare before you commit. Open the WebP next to the original JPG and look at the fine detail — hair, foliage, small text. At 85-90% you should struggle to tell them apart. If the WebP looks soft, convert again from the JPG at a higher quality rather than converting the WebP a second time.

The compatibility check

Every modern browser displays WebP — Chrome, Edge, Firefox and Safari have all supported it since 2020 — so images that stay on the web are safe. The risk is everything else: some upload forms, older apps, printers and older phones still expect JPG and will reject a WebP. Before converting a whole library, check where the pictures are going. A website or a modern app is fine; a government form, an ID upload or a print shop may not be.

One more habit worth keeping: do not convert a JPG to WebP and then back to JPG. Each lossy generation discards a little more, and the round trip costs quality twice. Convert from the original JPG each time, and keep the JPG as the master — the WebP is a lightweight copy for distribution, not a replacement for the original.

Questions people ask

Is WebP better than JPG?

For the web, usually yes: WebP produces a smaller file at the same visual quality, and it supports transparency, which JPG cannot. JPG still wins on universal acceptance — every device, form and app from the last thirty years opens a JPG, while WebP needs reasonably modern software.

Does converting JPG to WebP lose quality?

A little. WebP is a lossy encode of the JPG's pixels, so some data is discarded — but at 85-90% quality the loss is invisible in normal viewing. The JPG's own artefacts carry over, so the WebP can never look better than the JPG it came from.

Why can't I open the WebP file?

The program opening it is probably too old to know the WebP format. Any modern browser opens WebP — drag the file into a browser window to view it — and converting it back to JPG or PNG with our converter makes it readable everywhere again.
