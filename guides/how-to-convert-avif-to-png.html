How to convert AVIF to PNG

By Muhammad Shoaib · Published 17 November 2026 · Updated 17 November 2026

AVIF to PNG is the conversion for two situations: you need the picture in a format every app can open, and you need transparency or a lossless file to edit. PNG gives you both, at the cost of a much larger file than the tiny AVIF you started with. Here is how to convert, and when PNG is worth the size.

Why PNG, not JPG

PNG wins over JPG in exactly two ways, and they are the two reasons to choose it. First, transparency: PNG stores an alpha channel, so a graphic with a see-through background keeps it — JPG would fill it white. Second, losslessness: PNG stores every pixel exactly, so you can open, edit and save it as often as you like without losing anything further. JPG loses a little quality with every save.

Those two properties make PNG the right target when the picture is headed for editing, for a design tool, or for any workflow where the background must stay transparent. For a plain photo going to a form, JPG is the smaller and simpler choice — AVIF to JPG covers that case.

The size trade, honestly

The PNG will be much larger than the AVIF — often several times larger, sometimes far more. That is not a fault in the conversion. AVIF is one of the most efficient image formats ever made, and PNG is one of the least compressed: it stores every pixel with only modest compression. Going from one extreme to the other is bound to grow the file dramatically.

Expect it before you convert, and do not let the size alarm you. The PNG is a working file, not a distribution file: use it for the edit, the upload or the app that demands PNG, and keep the tiny AVIF as the archive copy. If the size is genuinely a problem — a strict upload limit, for example — AVIF to JPG gives a far smaller result, at the cost of transparency and losslessness.

How to convert AVIF to PNG

  1. In your browser — fastest, nothing to install. Use the AVIF to PNG Converter: drop one or more AVIF images onto the box, press Convert, and download each PNG or grab them all as a ZIP. There is no quality setting to choose, because PNG is lossless. The conversion runs on your own device, so the pictures are never uploaded anywhere.
  2. If the browser cannot decode AVIF, use GIMP. The converter reads AVIF through your browser, so it needs a current Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari 16+. If yours cannot decode the file, the tool says so plainly. In that case open the AVIF in GIMP (version 2.10.22 or later), choose File, then Export As, and save it as a PNG — GIMP decodes AVIF itself, on any computer.
  3. Check transparency if it matters. Open the converted PNG and view it against a coloured background. If the background shows through the transparent areas, transparency survived — it should, since both formats carry an alpha channel.
  4. Keep the AVIF as the compact archive. The PNG is for compatibility and editing. The AVIF is the better long-term copy: far smaller, and supported by more software every year. Keep both, and convert from the AVIF again if you ever need another format.

When to keep the AVIF

If the destination already accepts AVIF, do not convert at all. Every modern browser displays AVIF, so pictures that stay on the web are fine as they are — converting them only makes the files bigger for no benefit. The same applies to your own storage: AVIF is the most space-efficient way to keep a photo today.

Convert when something demands it: a form, an old app, an editing workflow, a recipient whose software cannot open AVIF. Those are real needs, and PNG answers them completely. But a conversion done 'just in case' is a conversion that only costs disk space.

Questions people ask

Does converting AVIF to PNG keep transparency?

Yes. Both formats store an alpha channel, so transparent areas in the AVIF stay transparent in the PNG. Check the result against a coloured background to confirm — if the colour shows through, transparency survived.

Why is the PNG so much bigger than the AVIF?

AVIF is one of the most efficient formats ever made and PNG is one of the least compressed, so the gap between them is large by design. A PNG several times the size of the AVIF is the normal, expected result — not a problem with the conversion.

My browser can't decode AVIF — what do I do?

Use GIMP, the free editor: version 2.10.22 and later open AVIF on Windows, Mac and Linux, and can export it as PNG. Alternatively, update your browser or try a different one — current Chrome, Edge, Firefox and Safari 16+ all decode AVIF.
