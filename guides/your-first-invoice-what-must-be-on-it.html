Your first invoice: what must be on it

Your first invoice is not a formality — it is the document that turns finished work into money. Get it right and a client pays without a single question. Leave out one detail and the same invoice can sit unpaid for weeks while emails go back and forth. Here is exactly what must be on it, and the mistakes first-timers make most.

What an invoice actually does

An invoice is two things at once: a request for payment and a record of what was agreed. It says who did the work, who it was for, what was delivered, how much it costs and when payment is due. For the client, it is the paper they need to approve and process the payment. For you, it is proof — if a client later says "I thought that was included" or "I never agreed to that price", the invoice is the document that settles it.

That is why an invoice is worth doing properly from the very first one. A clear invoice gets paid faster because there is nothing to query: the client can see the work, the rate and the total in one glance, and the payment goes straight into their payment queue. A vague invoice does the opposite — it starts a conversation, and conversations cost days.

The 8 things every invoice must have

  1. Your business name and contact details. Your name (or business name), email and phone number. The client needs to know exactly who they are paying, and how to reach you if something is unclear.
  2. Your client's name and contact details. The person or company being billed, with an address if you have one. An invoice addressed to the wrong name — or to no one in particular — is easy for a finance department to ignore.
  3. A unique invoice number. Every invoice gets its own number, in sequence: INV-001, INV-002, and so on. It is how you and the client refer to this bill in every later email, and how you find it in your own records at tax time.
  4. Issue date and due date. The date you sent the invoice, and the date payment is due. Without a due date, "pay soon" means different things to different people; most freelancers use 7–14 days.
  5. An itemised list of what you are charging for. One line per deliverable, not one lump sum for everything. "Logo design" is not an item — "Logo design, 2 concepts plus 1 revision" is.
  6. Quantity, rate and amount per line. Hours or units, the rate for each, and the line total. The client should be able to check your maths without asking you.
  7. Subtotal, tax or discount, total and amount due. Show the working: subtotal, minus any discount, plus any tax, equals the total. If part of the invoice is already paid, show the balance due as its own line so there is no doubt about what is left.
  8. How to pay. Bank details, a payment link, or a QR code the client can scan — written on the invoice itself, not buried in a separate email. If paying you takes effort, payment gets delayed.

How to write the itemised part

The itemised list is where most first invoices go wrong, because it is where vague wording hides. "Services rendered — Rs 25,000" tells the client nothing: they cannot tell what they are paying for, and if they dispute it, you have no written record of the deliverable either. Write each line as if a stranger had to understand it: what was delivered, when, and at what rate. "Product photos — 12 edited images, delivered 4 November — Rs 12,000" is a line nobody can argue with, because it names the work, the quantity and the date in one breath.

Keep the same rule for time-based work. "10 hours at Rs 2,000/hour, 3–7 November" is checkable; "Work done — Rs 20,000" is not. If the client agreed a fixed price in advance, write the agreed scope next to it anyway — "Website homepage redesign, as quoted 28 October" — so the invoice and the quote match each other exactly.

Payment terms that actually get you paid

Three choices decide how fast you get paid: the due date, the payment method and what happens if payment is late. Set a real due date rather than leaving payment open-ended — "due within 14 days of issue" is a deadline, "pay when you can" is a suggestion. Offer the easiest payment method the client already uses: a bank transfer for businesses, a payment link or a scannable QR code for everyone else. A QR code on the invoice that opens your payment page removes the last excuse for delay — the client points their phone at the paper and pays. If you want to charge a late fee, say so on the invoice before the work starts, not after the payment is already late — and check the rules where you live, because late-fee and tax rules differ by country. The same goes for tax: if you must add tax, show it as its own line so the client can see exactly what is tax and what is your fee. An invoice that hides its maths is an invoice that gets questioned.

Mistakes first-timers make

The most common first-invoice mistakes are small, and all of them are avoidable. Sending the invoice as an editable Word file instead of a PDF, so the client can change it and the layout breaks on their computer. Reusing an invoice number, or having no numbering at all, so neither side can find the bill later. Forgetting the due date entirely. Doing the tax or discount maths in your head and getting the total wrong by a few hundred — the client spots it, trust drops, and the payment waits while you correct it. And the quietest mistake of all: not keeping a copy. Save every invoice you send, with its number and date, from the very first one; at tax time, that folder is the difference between an afternoon of work and a week of searching.

Questions people ask

Do I need a registered business to send an invoice?

In most places, no. An invoice is a request for payment, not a company registration, and a freelancer can usually invoice under their own name. Tax and registration rules differ by country, so check what applies where you live before your first invoice — and keep a copy of every invoice you send, whatever your setup.

What if my client does not pay by the due date?

Send one polite reminder first: restate the invoice number, the amount, the original due date and exactly how to pay. Most late payments are forgetfulness, not refusal, and a clear reminder fixes them. If the client still does not respond, follow up again in writing and keep every message — a dated paper trail is what protects you if the delay turns into a dispute.

Should I send an invoice as a PDF or a Word file?

Always PDF. A PDF keeps your layout exactly as you made it on every device, it cannot be quietly edited by the receiver, and it looks professional. A Word file can be changed after you send it, it can reflow on a different computer, and it looks unfinished — the opposite of what you want the first time a client sees your billing.
