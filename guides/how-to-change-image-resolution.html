How to change image resolution

By Muhammad Shoaib · Published 17 November 2026 · Updated 17 November 2026

“Change the resolution” usually means one of two things: give the image more pixels for print, or fewer for screen. Here is what resolution actually means, why DPI is not what you think, and how to change it properly.

Resolution is pixels; DPI is a note

An image's resolution is its pixel dimensions: a 2400 × 3000 image has 7.2 million pixels, whatever any other number says. DPI — dots per inch — is not a property of the image at all. It is a small note inside the file telling a printer how large to make those pixels on paper.

That note changes nothing about the picture. A 1200 × 1800 image marked 72 dpi and the same image marked 300 dpi are the same image — the same pixels, the same quality. The 300 dpi note only tells the printer to make it 4 × 6 inches instead of 16 × 25. So when a form asks for “300 dpi”, what it really needs is enough pixels: 300 pixels for every inch of the printed size. Change the pixels, and the DPI takes care of itself.

Print sizes at 300 dpi

For a sharp print, aim for 300 pixels per inch of printed size. A 4 × 6 inch print needs 1200 × 1800 pixels; 5 × 7 needs 1500 × 2100; 8 × 10 needs 2400 × 3000; an A4 page needs 2480 × 3508. Each number is simply the print size multiplied by 300 — and each is a preset in the browser tool below.

You can go lower and still get an acceptable print. 200 dpi looks fine on a wall poster viewed from a distance, and 150 dpi is passable for a large banner — the further the viewer stands, the fewer pixels per inch the eye can resolve. But there is no way to add detail that was never captured: enlarging a small image to print size spreads its pixels thinner, and the print comes out soft.

Screen sizes and why DPI does not matter there

Screens ignore DPI entirely. A phone, a laptop and a projector all show the same pixels, just at different physical sizes — the DPI note in the file has no effect on any of them. For screens, the only question is pixel dimensions: 1920 × 1080 for Full HD, 3840 × 2160 for 4K. Anything larger is wasted; anything smaller looks soft when shown full-screen.

That is why “72 dpi for web” is a myth that refuses to die. It comes from the early days of the Macintosh, when screens really did show about 72 pixels per inch — but modern screens range from 100 to over 400. A web image's sharpness depends only on its pixel dimensions versus the size it is displayed at. Save your images at the pixel size they will be shown, and forget DPI for anything that stays on a screen.

How to change image resolution

  1. In your browser — fastest, nothing to install. Use the Image Resolution Converter: drop the image in, pick a preset (4×6, 5×7, 8×10, A4 at 300 dpi, Full HD or 4K) or type exact pixels, and download. The tool changes pixel dimensions and says so plainly.
  2. In GIMP. Open the image, choose Image, then Scale Image, and type the new width and height. Keep the chain link closed to preserve the aspect ratio. GIMP is free and offers a choice of interpolation methods for the resampling.
  3. On a Mac, with Preview. Open the image in Preview, choose Tools, then Adjust Size, and type the new dimensions. Preview also shows a Resolution field — that is the DPI note, and changing it alone does not change the image. Change the pixel dimensions, not just that number.
  4. Check the result. Open the resized file and confirm its dimensions with the Image Dimension Checker, and view it at 100% to judge sharpness. If it looks soft, the original was too small for the target size — no resizing method can invent the missing detail.

Why enlarging cannot add detail

Resizing down is safe: the software averages pixels together, and the result stays sharp. Resizing up is the opposite: the software must invent pixels it has never seen, and the usual methods — bilinear, bicubic — can only smooth and interpolate. The result is bigger, but softer: edges blur, textures wash out, and fine detail turns to mush.

AI upscaling is the exception, because it does not interpolate — it reconstructs, using patterns learned from millions of images to guess what the missing detail should look like. It is still a guess, and it can invent the wrong texture, but for photos it usually beats plain enlargement by a wide margin. The AI Image Upscaler on this site does exactly that, in your browser.

Questions people ask

Does changing DPI change my image?

No. DPI is only a note in the file telling a printer how large to make the pixels. Changing the note alone changes nothing about the picture — only the pixel dimensions matter.

What size do I need for a 4×6 print?

1200 × 1800 pixels — 4 inches times 300 pixels per inch, by 6 inches times 300. That is the preset for it in the browser tool.

Can I make a low-resolution image high resolution?

You can make it larger in pixels, but plain resizing cannot add detail — the result is bigger, not sharper. AI upscaling reconstructs detail and is the better option for small images.
