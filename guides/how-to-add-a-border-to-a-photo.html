How to add a border to a photo

A border is the simplest finishing touch a photo can get: it frames the picture, separates it from the page, and makes it look deliberate. Here is how to add a border to a photo — how thick it should be, which colour to choose, and four ways to do it, starting with the fastest.

Why borders make photos look finished

A photo without a border ends exactly where its pixels end, and on a white page or a white feed that edge disappears — a bright photo melts into the background and looks unfinished. A border draws a line around the picture and tells the eye where the photo stops and the page begins. It is a small change with an outsized effect: the same photo looks like a snapshot without a border and like a print with one.

Borders also give a set of photos a shared identity. A feed where every image has the same white border looks curated; a portfolio where every picture carries the same thin black frame looks like a gallery. The border becomes a signature — people recognise your posts before they read them. And for printing, a border is practical as well as pretty: it gives fingers somewhere to hold the print without touching the image, and it gives a frame a clean edge to sit against.

How thick should a border be

Thickness is relative to the image. On a 500-pixel profile picture, a 20-pixel border is already bold; on a 4000-pixel print, the same border is a hairline. A good starting point is about 2% of the image's longest side — 20 pixels on a 1000-pixel photo — and then adjust by eye. If the border disappears at normal viewing size, go thicker; if it is the first thing you notice, go thinner.

The purpose sets the range. Social posts and thumbnails look best with a thin border of 10 to 30 pixels: enough to define the edge, not enough to shrink the picture. Posters, certificates and formal portraits can carry much more — 50 to 100 pixels reads as a deliberate frame rather than an accident. Whatever you choose, keep it consistent across a set: mixed border thicknesses look like a mistake, while one thickness everywhere looks like a decision.

Choosing a border colour

White is the default for a reason: it reads as a clean frame on any background, suits any photo, and never competes with the picture. Black is the gallery choice — formal, and it makes colours look richer by contrast. A brand colour turns the border into a signature, and it is the strongest option for a feed or a shop where recognition matters.

What to avoid is a colour that fights the photo. A bright red border around a green landscape, or a yellow border around a blue sea, pulls the eye to the frame instead of the picture. When in doubt, sample a colour from the photo itself: a muted tone from the shadows or the sky ties the border to the image without shouting. And remember that the border is part of the file — it will print exactly as it looks on screen, so check the preview at full size before downloading.

How to add a border to a photo

  1. In your browser — fastest, nothing to install. Use the Image Border Maker: drop the photo in, drag the thickness slider, pick a colour, and download. The border is added around the outside, so the photo itself is never cropped.
  2. In Canva, for designs. Open your photo in a Canva design, then use Elements to search for a frame, or place the photo on a coloured background slightly larger than the image — the visible background becomes the border. Canva is free for this, and it suits posts where the border is part of a larger design.
  3. In GIMP, for precise work. Open the image, choose Image, then Canvas Size, and increase the width and height by twice the border thickness. Set the canvas fill to your border colour, or fill the new area afterwards with the bucket tool. GIMP is free and gives exact pixel control.
  4. On your phone, with Snapseed. Open the photo in Snapseed, tap Tools, then Frames, and choose a border style and width. Snapseed is free on iPhone and Android, and its frames include textured and coloured options beyond a plain border.

Borders for print vs screen

For screen, a border is a design choice and any thickness works. For print, remember that the border is part of the printed size: a 4 × 6 inch print with a thick border has less room for the photo itself. Printers also trim a millimetre or two from the edge, so a very thin border can come out uneven — for prints, use at least 15 to 20 pixels of border, or none at all.

One more practical point: a border changes the file's dimensions. A 1000 × 800 photo with a 20-pixel border becomes 1040 × 840. If the destination needs an exact size — a form, a listing, a profile picture — add the border first, then resize the result to the required dimensions with the Resize Image tool.

Questions people ask

Will a border cover part of my photo?

Not with the browser tool here — it adds the border around the outside, so the whole photo stays visible and the file grows by twice the thickness on each side. Some apps draw the border inside the image instead, which does cover the edges; check which kind yours is before saving.

What thickness looks best?

About 2% of the image's longest side is a good start — 20 pixels on a 1000-pixel photo. Thin borders suit social posts; thicker ones suit posters and formal prints.

Can I add a border to a transparent PNG?

Yes, but the border frames the full rectangle, including transparent areas. For a border that follows a cut-out shape, add a shadow instead with the Image Shadow Maker, which follows the image's outline.
