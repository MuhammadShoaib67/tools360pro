How to Calculate EMI

By Muhammad Shoaib · Published 17 November 2026 · Updated 17 November 2026

EMI — the Equated Monthly Instalment — is the fixed amount you pay each month towards a loan. It comes from a standard formula using the loan amount, the monthly interest rate and the number of payments. This guide shows the formula, works through an example, and explains the amortisation schedule that shows where each payment goes.

The EMI formula

The standard formula is EMI equals P times r times one plus r to the power of n, divided by one plus r to the power of n minus one — where P is the loan amount, r is the monthly interest rate, and n is the number of monthly payments. The formula spreads the loan and its interest evenly across the tenure, so every monthly payment is the same size even though the split between interest and principal changes each month.

The rate needs converting first: an annual rate of 10% becomes a monthly rate of 10 divided by 12 divided by 100, which is 0.00833. The tenure needs converting too: 5 years is 60 monthly payments. With those two conversions done, the formula gives the monthly EMI directly.

A worked example

Take a loan of 100,000 at 10% per year for one year. The monthly rate is 0.00833 and the number of payments is 12. Plugging those into the formula gives a monthly EMI of about 8,792. Over twelve months you pay about 105,499 in total, of which about 5,499 is interest and 100,000 is the loan itself.

You can check this with the calculator: enter 100,000, 10% and 1 year, and the EMI, total interest and total payment appear at once, with the full month-by-month schedule below. The schedule is where the formula comes alive — it shows the interest part shrinking and the principal part growing with every payment.

Calculate EMI in four steps

  1. Note the loan amount. This is the sum you want to borrow — the principal. It is the P in the formula.
  2. Convert the interest rate to a monthly rate. Divide the annual rate by 12 and then by 100. An annual rate of 10% becomes a monthly rate of 0.00833.
  3. Convert the tenure to months. Multiply years by 12 and add any extra months. Five years is 60 payments; 18 months is 18 payments. Then open the EMI Calculator and enter the three numbers.
  4. Read the EMI and the schedule. The calculator shows the monthly EMI, the total interest and the total payment, plus a full amortisation schedule showing how each payment splits between principal and interest.

Reading the amortisation schedule

Interest is charged on the outstanding balance, which is highest at the start of the loan. On the 100,000 example above, the first month's interest is 100,000 times 0.00833, which is 833 — so of the first EMI of about 8,792, only 7,958 repays the loan itself. By the final month, the balance is small, the interest is a few dozen, and almost the whole EMI goes to principal.

This is why extra payments early in a loan save the most interest. Any amount you pay above the EMI goes straight to the principal, which shrinks the balance that future interest is charged on. The schedule shows the split month by month, so you can see exactly where each payment goes — and how much an extra payment would save.

What changes your EMI

Three things decide the size of your EMI: the amount borrowed, the interest rate, and the tenure. A bigger loan or a higher rate raises the monthly payment; a longer tenure lowers it, because the same debt is spread over more months. But a longer tenure raises the total interest — you pay less each month and more overall.

Rates themselves come in two kinds. A fixed rate stays the same for the whole tenure, so your EMI never changes; a floating rate moves with the market, so your EMI can rise or fall. Fixed rates are easier to budget around, floating rates can be cheaper if rates fall — but they can also cost more. This calculator assumes a fixed rate for the whole tenure, which is how most EMI quotes are given.

Questions people ask

What does EMI stand for?

Equated Monthly Instalment: the fixed amount you pay every month towards a loan, covering both interest and principal.

What is the EMI formula?

EMI equals P times r times one plus r to the power of n, divided by one plus r to the power of n minus one, where P is the loan amount, r is the monthly rate and n is the number of payments.

Why is the first EMI mostly interest?

Interest is charged on the full outstanding balance, which is highest at the start. As the balance falls, more of each EMI goes to the principal.

Does a longer tenure mean less total interest?

No, the opposite. A longer tenure lowers the monthly payment but raises the total interest, because you pay interest for longer.
