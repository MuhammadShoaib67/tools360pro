How to convert MP4 to MP3 free online

By Muhammad Shoaib · Published 27 November 2026 · Updated 27 November 2026

An MP4 file is mostly picture. When all you want is the sound — a lecture to listen to on a walk, a song trapped in a phone video, a podcast recorded as video — converting it to MP3 keeps the part that matters in a file that plays everywhere, at roughly a tenth of the size. Here is how it works, which bitrate to pick, and how to do it free in your browser with nothing uploaded.

What converting MP4 to MP3 actually does

MP4 is a container, MP3 is audio only

MP4 is a container: one file holding a video track, an audio track and sometimes subtitles, all packaged together. The video track is usually more than 90% of the file's size. MP3 is the opposite — a single audio track and nothing else, in a format every phone, car stereo, music app and smart speaker on earth can play. Converting MP4 to MP3 means opening the container, taking out the audio track, and saving it on its own. The picture is thrown away, which is exactly the point: a one-hour lecture that was a 500 MB video becomes a 50–60 MB audio file you can carry anywhere.

The sound is re-encoded, not copied

Our converter does not just lift the audio out unchanged — your browser decodes the sound and encodes it again as MP3 at the bitrate you choose. That re-encoding always loses a tiny amount of detail, because MP3 compression works by discarding sounds the human ear barely notices. In practice the loss is so small that most people cannot hear it, especially at 192 or 320 kbps. The honest trade is this: you give up the picture and a whisper of audio detail, and in return you get a file ten times smaller that plays on anything.

Because the whole job happens in your browser tab, the file is never uploaded to any server. That matters for private recordings — an interview, a family video, a voice memo — because the audio never leaves your device at any point in the process.

Choosing the right bitrate

Bitrate is how much data each second of sound gets, and it decides both the quality and the file size. A rough rule: at 128 kbps, one minute of audio is about 1 MB; at 192 kbps it is about 1.4 MB; at 320 kbps about 2.4 MB. An hour-long lecture is therefore around 58 MB at 128 kbps, or around 144 MB at 320 kbps — a real difference on a phone with limited storage.

Speech: 128 kbps is enough

Speech — lectures, interviews, voice notes, audiobooks — stays perfectly clear at 128 kbps. Voices use a narrow range of frequencies, so there is little detail for the encoder to lose, and going higher mostly buys a bigger file. If the recording is a talk you want to listen to rather than a performance you want to enjoy, 128 kbps is the sensible choice.

Music: 192 or 320 kbps

Music deserves more. 192 kbps is a good all-round choice for most videos, and 320 kbps keeps the most detail for songs you will actually listen to — the cymbals, the room sound, the quiet parts. The preview lets you hear the difference before you commit, which is the honest way to choose: your ears, not a number on a chart. One thing no bitrate can do is add quality the original never had — if the video's sound was recorded quietly, or through a phone microphone in a noisy room, the MP3 will faithfully keep it that way at any setting.

When converting makes sense

Lectures and podcasts

A class recording or a video podcast is usually watched once and listened to many times. As MP3 it goes on your phone, plays with the screen off, works in the car, and costs a tenth of the storage. Students do this constantly: record the lecture as video, convert it, and listen to it again on the bus.

Music from videos

Sometimes the only copy of a song is inside a video — a live performance, a wedding recording, a clip a friend sent. Extracting the MP3 puts it in your music library, where it belongs, instead of leaving it trapped in a video app. Just make sure you have the right to keep it: converting a video you recorded yourself is fine, but ripping music from videos you do not own is a copyright problem, not a technical one.

Voice notes and interviews

Phone videos are often just a way to capture sound — an interview, a meeting, a child's first words. As MP3, the audio is easy to share in a chat, attach to an email, or archive alongside your notes, without sending a huge video file that nobody wants to download.

Limits worth knowing before you start

Some files will not convert

The conversion relies on your browser's own audio decoder, so a file only fails if your browser cannot play it. MKV files and videos with unusual audio codecs, like AC3, are the common exceptions — if the video will not play in your browser, it will not convert either. Re-saving it as MP4 in a video app first usually fixes that.

Very long recordings need splitting

The conversion happens in your tab's memory, so everyday clips work best — up to about 100 MB or 30–60 minutes of audio is comfortable on most devices. A two-hour recording may run out of memory; splitting it into parts first, then converting each part, avoids the problem entirely.

The video is gone — keep the original

An MP3 carries sound only, so the picture is discarded for good in the converted file. If the video itself matters — the faces, the whiteboard, the slides — keep the original MP4 as well. Convert a copy, not the only copy, and you can always go back.

Questions people ask

Is my video uploaded to a server when I convert it?

No. Your browser reads the file, extracts its sound and builds the MP3 on your own device. The file never leaves your computer or phone, and nothing is stored anywhere.

Which bitrate should I choose?

128 kbps is plenty for speech, lectures and voice notes. 192 kbps is a good all-round choice for most videos. 320 kbps keeps the most detail for music — but it cannot add quality the original file never had.

Will the MP3 sound as good as the video?

Very close. The sound is re-encoded, which always loses a tiny amount of detail — pick 320 kbps to keep the loss smallest. The picture is discarded, of course: an MP3 carries sound only.

Can I convert MOV files to MP3 too?

Yes — MOV videos from phones and cameras convert the same way as MP4. Drop the file on the page, pick a bitrate and download the MP3. WebM videos and audio files in M4A, MP3, WAV and OGG work too.
