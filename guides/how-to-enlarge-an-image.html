How to enlarge an image

By Muhammad Shoaib · Published 17 November 2026 · Updated 17 November 2026

A photo too small for a listing, a logo too small for a slide, an old picture that needs to fill a bigger frame — enlarging is the fix, and it is worth knowing what it can and cannot do before you start. Here is how to enlarge an image, when a plain resize is enough, and when AI upscaling is the better call.

What enlarging can and cannot do

Enlarging stretches the pixels you already have over a larger area. A 500-pixel image enlarged to 200% becomes 1000 pixels wide, but it contains no more detail than the original — the new pixels are interpolated, which is a careful guess at what should sit between the old ones. The result looks softer than a photo that was 1000 pixels to begin with, and no honest tool can change that.

Small enlargements hold up well. Going to 125% or 150% is barely noticeable on most photos; 200% is visibly softer but perfectly usable for screens. Beyond 300%, the softness becomes obvious, especially on text and hard edges. Use the preview to judge: if the result looks acceptable at the size you need, it is acceptable.

When a bigger file is actually needed

The common reason is a minimum size: a marketplace that wants 1000-pixel product photos, a print service that needs 300 DPI, a form that rejects small uploads. Enlarging meets the requirement, and for display purposes a clean 2x enlargement is usually fine — the requirement is about dimensions, not about true detail.

The other reason is cropping headroom. Enlarge first, then crop the part you want: the cropped piece keeps more pixels than cropping the small original. And for pixel art and logos with hard edges, enlarging by an exact multiple (200%, 300%) keeps the edges cleaner than an odd percentage — the interpolation has a simpler job.

How to enlarge an image

  1. In your browser — fastest, nothing to install. Use the Image Enlarger: drop the image in, pick a scale up to 400% or type exact dimensions, and download. It is a plain resize — it says so, and it never invents detail.
  2. On Windows, with Photos. Open the image in the Photos app, click the three dots, then Resize image, and choose a size by pixels or percentage. Photos handles enlarging as well as shrinking.
  3. On a Mac, with Preview. Open the image in Preview, then choose Tools, then Adjust Size, and type the new width or height. Keep “Scale proportionally” ticked so the picture does not stretch.
  4. Check the result at the size you will use it. An enlargement that looks soft at 100% zoom may look fine at the size it will actually be displayed. Judge it in context, not pixel by pixel.

Enlarging for print

Print is where enlargement matters most, because printers measure quality in dots per inch. A 1000-pixel image printed at 300 DPI is about 8.5 cm wide; the same image at 150 DPI prints twice as wide but visibly softer. Enlarging to 200% doubles the printable size at the same DPI — which is why print services ask for specific pixel dimensions, and why a small photo needs enlarging before it can fill a page.

The honest limit still applies: enlargement does not add detail, so a 2x enlargement printed large looks softer than a native high-resolution photo. For a poster viewed from across a room that softness is invisible; for a fine-art print examined up close it is not. Match the enlargement to the viewing distance — a metre away forgives a great deal, and most print is viewed from further than that.

Enlarging vs AI upscaling

A plain resize does not add detail, and it does not claim to. AI upscaling is a different process: a model trained on millions of photos guesses what the missing detail should look like, and can genuinely sharpen a small photo. If you need the sharpest possible enlargement of a face or a product shot, use the AI Image Upscaler on this site instead.

The trade-off is speed and honesty. A plain resize is instant, works on any image, and never invents detail that was not there — which matters for documents, diagrams and screenshots, where an AI guess could change a number or a letter. For those, a plain enlarger is the right tool; for photos where sharpness matters most, AI is.

Questions people ask

Will enlarging make my image sharper?

No. Enlarging spreads existing pixels over more space; it cannot add detail. The result is softer than a photo taken at that size. For sharper results, try the AI Image Upscaler.

How much can I enlarge an image?

The browser tool goes up to 400% of the original size. Small enlargements (125–200%) hold up well; beyond 300% the softness becomes obvious, especially on text.

Does enlarging change the aspect ratio?

No. Width and height scale together, so the picture keeps its proportions. If you need a different shape, crop it afterwards with the Crop Image tool.
