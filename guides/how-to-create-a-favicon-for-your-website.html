How to Create a Favicon for Your Website

The favicon is the tiny mark in a browser tab — sixteen pixels wide, and the first thing people scan when they have twenty tabs open. What a favicon is — and where people actually see it

A favicon is the small icon that represents your website wherever a browser shows it: in the tab next to the page title, in the bookmarks bar, in the browser history, and on a phone's home screen when someone saves your site there. It is the smallest piece of your brand, and the one people see most often — every open tab carries it, every bookmark list is scanned by it.

Sites without a favicon get the browser's generic globe or blank page icon instead, which reads as unfinished. A favicon does not bring traffic by itself, but it makes a site recognisable in a crowded tab bar and trustworthy in a list of search results and bookmarks. For a new site it is one of the cheapest credibility wins there is: one small image, done once, visible everywhere the site appears.

What makes a good favicon

Simple, high-contrast images make the best favicons. Detail that looks fine at 512 px disappears at 16 px, so the test that matters is the smallest size: shrink your logo to 16 px and look at it. If you can still tell what it is, it will work. A single bold letter, a simple symbol or a strong shape survives the shrink; a full wordmark, a detailed illustration or a photograph usually does not.

Colour helps more than detail. A solid brand-colour background with a white symbol on it reads clearly at every size and stays recognisable in a dark tab bar as well as a light one. Avoid thin lines and small text — at 16 px they turn into grey noise. And keep the source image square: a wide logo gets squeezed or cropped, while a square one scales cleanly into every size in the set. If your logo is not square, put it on a square canvas with padding first, then generate.

Putting the icons on your site — and the caching trap

Generating the set is half the job; the other half is telling browsers where to find it. The tool gives you an HTML snippet with the link tags ready made — paste it into the head section of your pages and upload the icon files alongside them. Keep the file names and the snippet in step, or the browser will ask for an icon that is not there. Upload the files to the site root, the same folder as your homepage, and the favicon.ico will also be found automatically even without the tag.

One honest warning: browsers cache favicons aggressively, so a new icon sometimes refuses to appear even when everything is correct. A hard refresh usually fixes it; if not, clearing the browser cache or waiting a day does. Test in a private window, where the cache is fresh, before deciding something is broken. And if you are on a builder like WordPress, use its own site-icon setting instead of editing files by hand — the result is the same set of files, uploaded the platform's way.

Common mistakes to avoid

The first mistake is using a photo as the source. A photo at 16 px is a smudge — use a logo or a simple symbol instead, and test it at the smallest size before you ship it. The second mistake is shipping only one size. A single 16 px file leaves phones and home screens with nothing to show, so generate the full set even if the tab icon is the only one you care about today.

The third mistake is a file-name mismatch: the snippet says favicon-32x32.png and the uploaded file is called favicon-32.png, so the browser asks for an icon that is not there. Keep the names the tool gives you. And the last mistake is judging the result too fast — if the new icon does not appear, it is almost always the cache, not the files. Hard-refresh, wait, and check again before rebuilding the whole set.

Questions people ask

What size should a favicon be?

There is no single size: browsers and phones ask for several — 16, 32 and 48 px inside the favicon.ico file, 180 px for Apple touch icons, and 192 and 512 px for Android home-screen and PWA icons. Start from a square image of at least 512 × 512 px and generate the whole set at once.

Do I need a favicon.ico file, or is a PNG enough?

Modern browsers accept a PNG favicon, but favicon.ico is still the safe universal choice: it is a real multi-size container (16, 32 and 48 px in one file), and browsers and bookmarks fall back to it automatically. The Favicon Generator makes both, so ship the set rather than choosing one.

Why is my new favicon not showing?

Browsers cache favicons aggressively, so a new icon sometimes refuses to appear even when everything is correct. A hard refresh usually fixes it; if not, clear the browser cache or wait a day. Also check that the file names in your link tags match the files you actually uploaded.

Can I make a favicon from a photo?

You can, but a simple logo or symbol works far better. Fine detail and text disappear at 16 px, so a photo usually turns into an unrecognisable smudge in a browser tab. Logos and bold shapes survive the shrink; photos do not.
