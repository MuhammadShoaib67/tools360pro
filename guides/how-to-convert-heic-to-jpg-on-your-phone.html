How to convert HEIC to JPG on your phone

By Muhammad Shoaib · Published 25 November 2026 · Updated 25 November 2026

Your phone took the photo, but the form, the website or the person you sent it to cannot open it. That is the HEIC problem in one sentence: iPhones save photos as HEIC by default, some Android phones do the same, and much of the rest of the world still expects JPG. The fix takes about a minute, needs no app and no upload, and this guide covers it on both iPhone and Android — the browser method that works on any phone, the built-in tricks on each system, and the two settings that stop the problem coming back.

Method 1: the browser converter — works on any phone

The quickest route is our free HEIC to JPG tool, which runs entirely in your phone's browser — Safari on iPhone, Chrome on Android. There is nothing to install, no account to create and nothing uploaded: the conversion happens on your own phone, so even private family photos stay private.

  1. Open the HEIC to JPG tool in your browser. Any recent phone works — the tool runs in the browser, so there is nothing to install. If you will use it often, add the page to your home screen so it behaves like an app icon.
  2. Tap the upload box and pick the photos from your library. You can select several photos at once, and the tool converts them together.
  3. Choose JPG for the smallest compatible files, or PNG if you plan to edit the photo further. JPG is the right choice for almost everything: forms, emails, marketplace listings and sharing. PNG avoids any extra compression, but the files are larger.
  4. Press Convert photos, wait a moment, then download each JPG. When you converted a batch, grab them all at once with Download all as ZIP — far quicker than saving one photo at a time when a whole holiday album needs converting.

A JPG at the tool's default quality looks identical to the original at normal viewing size, and it opens everywhere: forms, email, marketplaces, Windows computers, older Android phones and any app that ever rejected the HEIC file. Keep the original HEIC files too — they are smaller and higher quality, so they are the better archive, and you can always convert them again later.

Method 2: the iPhone Files app trick (iOS 16 and later)

If you would rather not open a website, the iPhone has a built-in conversion hidden in the Files app. It works on iOS 16 and later, and it converts several photos at once:

  1. In Photos, select the photos, tap Share and choose Save to Files, and pick a folder.
  2. Open the Files app and go to that folder.
  3. Touch and hold a photo — or select several first — then choose Quick Actions, then Convert Image.
  4. Choose JPEG and a size. The JPG is saved next to the original HEIC.

That is genuinely free and genuinely built in, but it is also fiddly for more than a handful of photos, and the converted copy sits in Files rather than in your photo library. For a whole holiday album, the browser method above is faster — select the batch, convert once, download as a ZIP.

Method 3: on Android — view it first, convert when you need a JPG

On Android the situation is the opposite of the iPhone's: the photo often opens, so the problem hides until you actually need a JPG. Google Photos can display many HEIC photos, and the gallery app on newer Samsung phones opens them too. If all you wanted was to look at the picture, you are done — no conversion needed.

But do not mistake viewing for converting. Google Photos has no save-as-JPG button on Android, and saving the photo to your device keeps the original HEIC file. The moment you need to attach the photo to a form, upload it to a website or send it to an app that only accepts JPG, viewing cannot help — convert it with the browser method above. The same is true of Samsung Gallery on phones that open HEIC: viewing works, exporting a JPG copy does not.

One more Android route exists when the photo arrived from someone else: ask the sender to resend it as a JPG. An iPhone user sharing to a non-Apple device usually sends a JPG automatically, so a fresh share from their Photos app often fixes the file without any conversion at all.

Stop your phone saving HEIC in the first place

Converting fixes today's photos. These settings fix tomorrow's.

On iPhone, go to Settings, then Camera, then Formats, and choose Most Compatible. Every new photo is saved as JPG from that moment on. The trade is storage: JPG files are roughly twice the size of HEIC, so on a phone that is already short of space, think before you switch. The setting only affects new photos — everything already in your library stays HEIC.

A second iPhone setting handles the cable instead of the camera. Under Settings, then Photos, scroll to Transfer to Mac or PC and choose Automatic. Photos copied to a computer over a cable are then converted to JPG on the way, while the originals stay HEIC on your phone. It only affects cable transfers — sharing by email or message follows different rules — but if your usual route is a USB cable to a Windows PC, this one setting ends the problem.

On Samsung phones, the Camera app's advanced picture options include a HEIF toggle — switch it off and new photos save as JPG. Other Android phones that shoot HEIF have a similar option in their camera settings; if yours does not, the browser converter above is the fallback, and it costs you nothing.

When the photo still will not convert

A small number of files will not convert in any browser tool: 10-bit and HDR variants from some devices cannot be decoded by the browser library, and a damaged file fails too. If one photo in a batch refuses, convert the rest and deal with the stubborn one separately — the batch still converts normally, so one photo never blocks the others. For a single stubborn photo, ask the person who took it to share it again from their phone, or open it on the device that took it and take a screenshot of it at full size: a screenshot is a PNG, and it opens everywhere, though it will not match the original's resolution.

Whichever method you pick, the habit worth building is the same: convert once, keep the HEIC original as your archive, and send the JPG everywhere else. That way the format stops being something you think about, and the photo simply opens — on your phone, on a form, on a Windows computer, wherever it lands.

Questions people ask

Can I convert HEIC to JPG on my phone without an app?

Yes. Open our free HEIC to JPG tool in Safari on iPhone or Chrome on Android, pick the photos from your library and download the JPGs — nothing is installed and nothing is uploaded, because the conversion runs on your phone. The iPhone also has a built-in route through the Files app on iOS 16 and later, but the browser method is faster for a batch.

Why won't my HEIC photo open on my Android phone?

Support varies by phone maker and Android version. Google Photos can display many HEIC photos and Samsung Gallery opens them on newer phones, but viewing is not converting: you usually cannot edit the photo, attach it to a form or share it into an app that expects JPG. Convert it to JPG once with a browser converter and the JPG works everywhere.

How do I stop my iPhone saving photos as HEIC?

Go to Settings, then Camera, then Formats, and choose Most Compatible. New photos are saved as JPG from then on; photos already in your library stay HEIC. If you only want the conversion to happen when photos leave the phone, leave the camera on High Efficiency and set Transfer to Mac or PC to Automatic under Settings, then Photos, instead.

Does converting HEIC to JPG lose quality?

A little, in theory — JPG is a lossy format, while HEIC stores more detail in less space. In practice, at the tool's default 92% quality, the converted photo looks identical to the original at normal viewing size, and it opens on every device and website. Keep the original HEIC file as your archive and use the JPG for sharing, forms and uploads.
