How to Write a Meta Title That Gets Clicked

Your meta title is the blue clickable headline in Google's search results, and it is the single most influential on-page element for both rankings and clicks. A good title tells Google what the page is about and tells the searcher why this result is worth clicking — in about 60 characters. This guide shows you how to write titles that do both.

What makes a meta title work

A working title does two jobs at once. For Google, it states the page's topic clearly, with the primary keyword near the front — Google weights early words more heavily, and searchers scanning results do the same. For the searcher, it gives a reason to click: a clear benefit, a specific answer, or a number that promises something concrete. A title that only does one of those jobs is half a title.

Two more rules matter. Every page needs its own unique title — duplicates confuse Google about which page to rank and split your clicks between pages. And the title should match the search intent behind the query: a how-to title for a how-to query, a product title for a buying query. Put the brand at the end, not the start, unless the brand itself is what people are searching for.

How long should a meta title be?

Google truncates titles by pixels, not characters — around 600 pixels wide. In practice that means roughly 50 to 60 characters is safe, but character count is only a rough guide: wide letters like W and M and capital letters eat far more width than narrow ones like i and l. A 55-character title full of wide letters can truncate, while a 62-character title of narrow letters can fit.

A title that truncates mid-word looks unfinished and wastes the space Google gave you. Check the pixel width, not just the character count, before you publish — and remember that Google may rewrite your title anyway if it thinks a different title matches the query better.

Write a meta title in five steps

  1. Find the page's primary keyword. The phrase the page actually answers, in the words a searcher would use. One page, one primary keyword — if a page is trying to rank for five phrases, it will rank well for none of them.
  2. Draft three or four options. Templates help: the keyword plus a benefit, a how-to with the year, a number plus an outcome. The Meta Title Generator builds these from your keyword so you can compare options side by side instead of staring at a blank field.
  3. Check the length. Under 60 characters and under 600 pixels. If a draft is too long, shorten it by cutting filler words — very, really, best-ever — not by cutting the keyword or the benefit.
  4. Make it sound like a human wrote it. Read it aloud. If it reads like keyword stuffing, rewrite it. A title that a person would say out loud is almost always a title that earns clicks.
  5. Add it to your page. The title lives in the <title> element inside the head of the page. On WordPress, use the SEO title field in your SEO plugin. After publishing, check how it renders in search — a Google rewrite usually means the original title did not match the query well.

Meta title mistakes

Keyword stuffing is the oldest mistake and still the most common: a title like Buy Shoes, Cheap Shoes, Shoes Online reads like spam to people and to Google. Duplicate titles across pages are next — every page deserves its own title, even if the pages are similar. Titles that do not match the page content get rewritten or ignored by Google, which means you lose control of your own listing.

ALL CAPS wastes pixel width and looks like shouting. And clickbait that the page does not deliver — a title promising a free tool on a page that asks for an email — costs trust and sends visitors straight back to the results, which Google notices.

Titles for different page types

A homepage title should say what you do and for whom, with the brand name included. A product page title leads with the product name, adds one key attribute, and ends with the brand. A blog post title leads with the benefit or the answer and carries the keyword naturally. A category page title names the category and adds a differentiator — free, no signup, instant — that sets it apart from the ten other results saying the same thing.

Questions people ask

How many characters should a meta title be?

Aim for 50 to 60 characters. Google truncates titles at around 600 pixels, so character count is only a rough guide: wide letters take more space than narrow ones.

Should the keyword go at the start of the title?

Usually yes. Putting the primary keyword near the front helps Google understand the page and helps searchers spot the relevance at a glance. Keep it natural, not forced.

Why did Google change my title?

Google rewrites titles when it thinks a different title matches the search better, often pulling text from the page heading or content. A rewrite is a hint that your title did not match the query intent well.

Can two pages have the same title?

They can, but they should not. Duplicate titles confuse Google about which page to rank and split your clicks. Give every page a unique title.
