How to calculate BMI: formula, chart and what your number means

Body Mass Index is one number made from your height and weight. It takes thirty seconds to work out, it is used by doctors and health services all over the world — and it is widely misunderstood. Here is the formula, a worked example, the chart, and the honest limits of what that number can tell you.

What BMI actually measures

BMI compares your weight with your height. It was designed in the 1830s by the Belgian statistician Adolphe Quetelet as a way to describe populations, not to judge individuals — and that origin still matters. A single number cannot measure body fat, fitness, diet or health on its own. What it can do is sort people into broad bands, and across large groups those bands track closely with health risk. That is why clinics, insurers and public health programmes keep using it: it is cheap, it needs only two measurements, and it is a useful first check, not a final verdict.

Two things follow from that. First, a BMI outside the healthy band is a reason to look closer, not a diagnosis. Second, a BMI inside the healthy band is not a clean bill of health either. Both points are worth keeping in mind before you calculate anything.

The BMI formula

There are two versions of the same formula — metric and imperial — and they give the same answer when the measurements are converted properly.

Metric: BMI = weight in kilograms ÷ (height in metres)². In other words, divide your weight by your height squared.

Imperial: BMI = 703 × weight in pounds ÷ (height in inches)². The 703 is just the conversion factor that makes the imperial version match the metric one.

A worked example makes it concrete. Take a person who weighs 70 kg and is 1.75 m tall. Square the height (1.75 × 1.75 = 3.0625), then divide: 70 ÷ 3.0625 = 22.9. In imperial units, the same person weighs about 154 lb and stands 5 feet 9 inches (69 inches) tall: 703 × 154 ÷ (69 × 69) = 108,262 ÷ 4,761 = 22.7. The small difference is rounding in the conversion, not a different formula.

The most common mistake is forgetting to square the height, or mixing units — centimetres with kilograms, or feet with pounds. How to calculate your BMI, step by step

  1. Measure your height properly. Stand barefoot against a wall, heels together, and mark the top of your head. Morning height is slightly taller than evening height, so measure at a consistent time of day.
  2. Weigh yourself on a flat, hard surface. Weigh in the morning, after using the bathroom and before eating, in light clothing, for the most consistent reading.
  3. Use the same units. Either kilograms with metres (or centimetres converted to metres), or pounds with inches. Do not mix the two systems in one calculation.
  4. Apply the formula. Square your height, divide your weight by that number — or enter both figures in the BMI Calculator and read the result.
  5. Read the category, then read the context. Compare your number with the chart below, and remember the limits described further down this page before drawing any conclusion.

The BMI chart for adults

These are the World Health Organization bands for adults aged 20 and over. They are the same for men and women.

When BMI gets it wrong

BMI treats all weight as the same, and bodies are not that simple. The people it misjudges most often are:

  • Athletes and very muscular people. Muscle is denser than fat, so a fit, muscular person can land in the overweight band while carrying very little body fat.
  • Older adults. Muscle is gradually lost with age while fat increases, so an older person can hold a healthy BMI and still carry too much fat for their health.
  • Children and teenagers. Growing bodies are assessed against age and sex percentile charts, not the adult bands — the adult chart simply does not apply.
  • Pregnant women. BMI is not used during pregnancy, because weight gain then is expected and necessary.
  • Some ethnic groups. Health risks can rise at lower BMIs in some populations, notably South Asian and East Asian groups, which is why some health services use lower cut-off points for them.

None of this makes BMI useless — it makes it a screening tool. A surprising result is a prompt to measure something else, not a verdict. The simplest second measure is waist circumference: for most adults, a waist above 94 cm (37 inches) for men or 80 cm (31.5 inches) for women signals extra risk even when BMI looks fine. Together, the two numbers tell a much better story than either one alone.

What to do with your number

If your BMI sits in the healthy band, the practical goal is to keep it there: regular movement, a balanced diet, enough sleep and a weigh-in every week or two so small drifts get caught early. If it sits above 25, the evidence points to gradual change rather than crash diets — a modest, sustained calorie reduction plus more daily activity moves the number slowly and keeps it moved. If it sits below 18.5, or if it has changed quickly without an obvious reason, that is worth a conversation with a doctor rather than a self-directed plan. Rapid, unexplained weight change in either direction is a symptom to check, not a target to chase.

Whatever your number is, measure it the same way each time and track the trend over months, not days. Daily weight swings of a kilogram or two are normal — water, food and the time of day all move the scales. The trend line is the truth; a single reading is just a dot on it.

Questions people ask

What is the formula for BMI?

In metric units, BMI equals weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared: kg / m². In imperial units, BMI equals 703 times weight in pounds divided by height in inches squared: 703 × lb / in². Both formulas give the same number when the measurements are converted correctly — for example, a person who is 70 kg and 1.75 m tall has a BMI of 22.9.

What is a healthy BMI for adults?

For adults, the World Health Organization defines a healthy BMI as 18.5 to 24.9. Below 18.5 is classed as underweight, 25 to 29.9 is overweight, and 30 or above is obese. These bands are the same for adult men and women. They are a population-level guide, not a diagnosis — a single number cannot say whether an individual person is healthy.

Is BMI accurate for everyone?

No. BMI only uses height and weight, so it cannot tell muscle apart from fat. A very muscular person can score as overweight while carrying little body fat, and an older adult can have a healthy BMI while carrying too much fat and too little muscle. BMI also works differently for children, who are assessed against age and sex percentiles instead of the adult bands, and it is not used during pregnancy. Use it as a first check, then add a second measure such as waist circumference.

How do I calculate BMI in pounds and inches?

Multiply your weight in pounds by 703, then divide by your height in inches squared. For example, a person who weighs 154 lb and is 5 feet 9 inches tall (69 inches) works out as 703 × 154 / 69², which is about 22.7.
