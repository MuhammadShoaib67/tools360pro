What a sleep cycle is

Sleep is not one long stretch of unconsciousness. It moves through stages — light sleep, deep sleep, and dreaming REM sleep — in cycles that last about 90 minutes on average. A full night of sleep is four to six of these cycles in a row, and the stages repeat in roughly the same order each time.

Ninety minutes is an average, not a rule. Cycles vary between people and across the night — early cycles have more deep sleep, later cycles have more dreaming — so the times this calculator gives are guides, not exact predictions. They are close enough to be useful, and far better than guessing.

Why waking mid-cycle feels worse

Waking up in the middle of a deep-sleep stage causes sleep inertia: that heavy, groggy feeling that can take half an hour to shake off. Waking at the end of a cycle, when sleep is light, feels much easier — even if you slept fewer hours in total. That is why six hours of sleep can feel better than seven: six hours is four full cycles, while seven hours interrupts the fifth.

The calculator works with this. It counts back or forward in whole 90-minute cycles from the time you enter, and adds 15 minutes for falling asleep — the average time it takes most people to drift off. The result is a set of times that land between cycles instead of inside one.

How to use the times

Aim for five or six cycles a night: 7.5 to 9 hours of sleep, which is the range most adults need. If the recommended times do not fit your schedule, four cycles — six hours — is a workable minimum for a night or two, but not a long-term habit. Three cycles is an emergency, not a plan.

Keep the times regular. Going to bed and waking at roughly the same times each day trains your body to complete its cycles on schedule, which matters more than any single night's calculation. And remember this is a timing guide, not medical advice — if you regularly struggle to sleep, or feel tired no matter how long you sleep, speak to a doctor.

Naps follow the same logic. A 20-minute nap stays in light sleep, so you wake easily; a 90-minute nap is one full cycle, so you also wake easily. A 45-minute nap is the worst of both, because it wakes you from deep sleep. If you nap, keep it short or make it a full cycle — the middle ground is where the grogginess lives.

Frequently asked questions

How long is a sleep cycle?

About 90 minutes on average, though cycles vary between people and across the night. The calculator uses 90 minutes as a working average.

Why does the calculator add 15 minutes?

Most people take around 15 minutes to fall asleep, so the cycles start 15 minutes after the bedtime you enter. If you fall asleep faster or slower, shift the times by a few minutes.

How many sleep cycles do I need?

Most adults need 5 to 6 cycles a night, which is 7.5 to 9 hours of sleep. The calculator highlights those options.

Is this medical advice?

No. The calculator is a simple timing guide based on average cycle lengths. If you have persistent trouble sleeping, speak to a doctor.

Is my data uploaded anywhere?

No. The calculation runs entirely in your browser using JavaScript. Nothing you enter is uploaded, stored or sent anywhere.

What time should I go to bed if I wake at 6am?

For a 6am wake-up, the calculator suggests 10:15pm for 5 cycles (7.5 hours) or 8:45pm for 6 cycles (9 hours) — both include the 15 minutes it takes to fall asleep. Enter your own wake-up time to get bedtimes for your schedule.

Why is waking up mid-cycle so groggy?

Because you are woken from deep sleep, and the body needs time to climb out of it. Waking between cycles means you surface from light sleep, which feels far easier — that is the whole idea behind timing your wake-up to the end of a cycle.

Can I use this calculator for shift work?

Yes — the calculator works from any bedtime or wake-up time, so an afternoon bedtime for a night shift works the same way as a night-time one. The cycles are the same 90 minutes; only the clock times change.
