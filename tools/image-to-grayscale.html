Why black and white still works

Colour can be a distraction. In a busy photo — a street scene, a crowded table, a garden — the eye jumps between bright colours instead of following the shapes and the light. Removing the colour leaves composition, texture and expression, which is why black and white portraits often feel more direct than the colour original.

Grayscale also flatters difficult light. Mixed indoor lighting throws yellow and green casts across a photo that no white-balance fix fully removes; in black and white, the casts simply disappear. Old faded prints, where the colour has shifted with age, often look better in grayscale than in their stained original colours.

Grayscale for printing and forms

The practical reason to convert is printing. Black and white prints cost less than colour on most printers and photocopiers, and many official forms, worksheets and handouts are printed in grayscale anyway — converting first lets you check that the result still reads clearly before you print.

The same applies to documents and diagrams. A coloured chart photographed for a report may print as a muddy grey mess if the colours are similar in brightness; converting to grayscale first shows you exactly what the print will look like, so you can raise the contrast if two shades merge into one.

Grayscale files are also smaller. A colour photo stores three channels of information; a grayscale version stores one. In practice the saving is modest for JPG, which already compresses colour efficiently, but for PNG the difference is real — a grayscale PNG of a scanned document can be a third the size of the colour original, which matters when you are emailing a batch of scans.

Grayscale vs desaturation

This tool converts with a proper luminance formula: green counts most, red next, blue least — matching how the human eye actually sees brightness. That is why a grayscale conversion looks natural, with skies and skin in believable tones, rather than a flat average of the three channels.

Desaturating an image to 0% saturation gives a similar result, and the Saturation Adjuster can do it — but a dedicated grayscale conversion is a single honest step with no slider to second-guess. The output is a true grayscale image, and saved as PNG it keeps every shade exactly.

Frequently asked questions

Are my images uploaded to a server?

No. The conversion runs on a canvas in your browser. Your images never leave your device.

Is grayscale the same as black and white?

Yes — the terms mean the same thing here. The image keeps all its shades of grey, from black to white; it is not reduced to two colours.

Will the grayscale version keep the same size?

Yes. Converting changes the colours, not the dimensions. A 2000-pixel-wide photo becomes a 2000-pixel-wide grayscale photo.

Why does my grayscale photo look darker than the original?

Colour carries brightness information, and some colours — especially reds — look brighter in colour than their grey equivalent. If the result feels dark, brighten the original first with the Brightness Adjuster.

Why can't I download in my browser?

This tool applies its effect with canvas filters, which Safari 17 and older do not support. The preview still works, but downloading needs Chrome, Edge, Firefox, or Safari 18+.

What formats can I download?

Keep the original format, or choose JPEG, WebP or PNG. PNG loses nothing; JPG makes the smallest files for photos.

Does grayscale keep the image dimensions?

Yes — the converted image keeps the same width and height as the original. Only the colour is removed; the size, shape and detail of the photo stay exactly as they were.

Why is there no intensity slider?

Because the tool is deliberately one-click: the conversion uses a fixed luminance formula, so there is nothing to adjust. If you want control over how much colour remains, the Saturation Adjuster tool lets you dial it down gradually.

Can I convert a photo to black and white for printing?
