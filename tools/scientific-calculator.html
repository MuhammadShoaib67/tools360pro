    How to use the Scientific Calculator

    1. Pick the angle mode first: DEG for school geometry, RAD for calculus and physics.
    2. Type an expression in the box — sin(90), 2^10, sqrt(144), 5! — or build it with the keypad. A live preview appears as you type.
    3. Press Calculate or Enter for the answer. Click any history entry to bring its expression back and edit it.

    Degrees or radians?

    The most common scientific-calculator mistake is not the maths — it is the angle mode. In degrees, a right angle is 90 and sin(90) = 1. In radians, the same angle is π/2 and sin(pi/2) = 1. The numbers are the same geometry, but a calculator in the wrong mode gives a confident wrong answer: sin(90) in radians is 0.894, which looks plausible enough to copy into homework unnoticed. This calculator starts in degrees, because that is what school work uses, and remembers your choice in this browser.

    As a rule of thumb: if the question talks about triangles, bearings or angles in degrees, use DEG. If it talks about π, arc length, calculus or waves, use RAD. The toggle only changes the six trig functions — sin, cos, tan and their inverses. Hyperbolic functions (sinh, cosh, tanh) do not take angles at all, so the mode leaves them alone. If an answer looks wrong, check the mode before doubting the maths.

    What you can type

    Expressions follow normal maths notation: + − * / for arithmetic, ^ for powers (2^10 = 1024), ! for factorials (5! = 120), and parentheses to group. Functions take their argument in brackets: sin(pi/2), log10(1000), sqrt(144), cbrt(27). The constants pi and e are built in, so 2*pi and e^2 work as written. The keypad inserts the same text, so tapping √ is identical to typing sqrt( — use whichever is faster.

    Two things worth knowing. First, log on the keypad means base 10 (it inserts log10), while ln is the natural log, base e — the distinction matters in chemistry and physics, where the two are used for different jobs. Second, everything is evaluated by a real maths parser on your device, not by the browser's JavaScript eval, and nothing is sent anywhere: the page works offline once loaded. For everyday sums, Percentage Calculator is quicker; for grades, try GPA Calculator and Final Grade Calculator.

    The history is the feature

    The most useful part of this calculator is not on the keypad — it is the history underneath it. Your last fifty calculations stay there in this browser, so a multi-step problem does not have to live in your head: work out one step, use its answer in the next, and scroll back to check an earlier figure whenever you need it.

    Clicking a history entry brings it back into the expression box, ready to edit or reuse — handy when only one number in a long expression was wrong and retyping the whole thing would waste five minutes. When the list gets crowded, the clear button removes them all at once, and nothing about the history ever leaves your device.

    Where each function earns its keep

    The functions on the keypad map to real jobs. Logarithms turn multiplication into addition, which is why pH in chemistry is a base-ten log scale and why sound levels use decibels. Factorials count arrangements — five objects can be ordered in 5! = 120 ways — which is the starting point of probability and combinatorics. Powers and roots handle growth, scaling and anything that compounds.

    Trig belongs to triangles, bearings and waves; the hyperbolic functions belong to engineering curves and the shape of a hanging cable. You do not need to memorise which is which — type the expression as you would write it, in ordinary maths notation, and let the parser sort out the order of operations. If an answer looks wrong, check the angle mode first: it is the usual suspect.

    Frequently asked questions

    Should I use degrees or radians?

    For school geometry, triangles and most homework, use degrees — sin(90) is 1. For calculus, physics and anything written with π, use radians — sin(pi/2) is 1. The toggle switches the trig functions; hyperbolic functions are unaffected because they do not take angles.

    What is the difference between log and ln?

    log is the base-10 logarithm (log(1000) = 3) and ln is the natural logarithm, base e (ln(e) = 1). log2 is the base-2 logarithm, used in computer science. The keypad's log button inserts log10, so it always means base 10.

    Can I type expressions instead of tapping buttons?

    Yes — click the input and type, for example sin(pi/2), 2^10, sqrt(144) or 5!. Use * for multiply, / for divide, ^ for powers and ! for factorials. Press Enter to calculate, Escape to clear.

    Does it work offline?

    Yes. Once the page has loaded, every calculation runs on your device — the maths engine is part of the page, not a server. You can keep using it with no connection.

    How does the history work?

    Your last 50 calculations are saved in this browser, newest first. Click any entry to put its expression back into the input so you can edit it and calculate again. Clear history removes them all.
