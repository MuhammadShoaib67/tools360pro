Resolution means pixels — DPI is only a note

“Resolution” is one of the most misunderstood words in imaging. An image's resolution is its pixel dimensions: a 2400 × 3000 image has 7.2 million pixels, whatever any other number says. DPI — dots per inch — is not a property of the image at all. It is a small note inside the file that tells a printer how large to make those pixels on paper.

That note changes nothing about the picture itself. A 1200 × 1800 image marked 72 dpi and the same image marked 300 dpi are the same image — the same pixels, the same quality. The 300 dpi note only tells the printer to make it 4 × 6 inches instead of 16 × 25. So when a form asks for “300 dpi”, what it really needs is enough pixels: 300 pixels for every inch of the printed size. This tool gives you those pixels, and its presets are calculated exactly that way.

Print sizes that actually work

For a sharp print, aim for 300 pixels per inch of printed size. A 4 × 6 inch print needs 1200 × 1800 pixels; 5 × 7 needs 1500 × 2100; 8 × 10 needs 2400 × 3000; an A4 page needs 2480 × 3508. Those are the presets in this tool — each one is the print size multiplied by 300.

You can go lower and still get an acceptable print: 200 dpi looks fine on a wall poster viewed from a distance, and 150 dpi is passable for a large banner. But there is no way to add detail that was never captured. Enlarging a small image to print size spreads its pixels thinner — it prints larger, not sharper. For genuinely small images, the AI Image Upscaler reconstructs detail instead of merely stretching pixels.

Screen sizes and aspect ratio

Screens do not use DPI at all — a phone, a laptop and a projector all show the same pixels, just at different physical sizes. For screens, the only question is pixel dimensions: 1920 × 1080 for Full HD, 3840 × 2160 for 4K. Anything larger is wasted on a screen; anything smaller looks soft when shown full-screen.

Keep the aspect ratio on unless you have a reason to stretch. Turning a 3:2 photo into a 4:3 frame by typing both numbers squashes the picture — faces get wider, circles become ovals. If a destination needs a different shape, crop to that shape with the Crop Image tool instead of stretching. Your original file is never changed — the download is always a new file.

Frequently asked questions

Are my images uploaded to a server?

No. Resizing runs on a canvas in your browser. Your images never leave your device.

Does this tool change the DPI of my image?

No — it changes pixel dimensions, which is what actually matters. DPI is only a small note in the file telling a printer how large to make the pixels; changing the note alone would not change the picture at all.

What size do I need for a 4×6 print?

1200 × 1800 pixels — 4 inches times 300 pixels per inch, by 6 inches times 300. The preset in this tool is exactly that size.

Can I make a small image high resolution?

You can make it larger in pixels, but enlarging cannot add detail the camera never captured — the result is bigger, not sharper. For small images, the AI Image Upscaler is a better try than plain resizing.

Why is my image stretched after converting?

That happens when “Keep aspect ratio” is off and both dimensions are typed in. Turn it on, change one side, and the other follows automatically — or crop to the shape you need instead of stretching.

What formats can I download?

Keep the original format, or choose JPEG, WebP or PNG. PNG loses nothing; JPG makes the smallest files for photos.

What is the difference between resolution and DPI?

Resolution is the pixel dimensions of the image — its width and height in pixels. DPI is a print setting that says how densely those pixels are printed. This tool changes the pixel dimensions; it does not edit DPI metadata, because for print what matters is having enough pixels.

Can I resize an image to 4K?

Yes — pick the 4K UHD preset and the image becomes 3840 by 2160 pixels. That suits a large display or a 4K frame, though enlarging a small image to 4K will not add detail that was not there.

What happens if I turn off keep aspect ratio?

The image is forced to the exact width and height you type, which stretches or squashes it if the shape does not match. Keep the toggle on unless you need an exact size and accept the stretch — for most uses, the aspect ratio should stay locked.
