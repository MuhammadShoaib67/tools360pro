  1. Type or paste the text you want to hear into the box, or click Sample to load an example sentence.

How browser text to speech works

Your browser has a built-in speech engine, and this tool simply hands it your text. The voices come from your device: Windows, macOS, Android and iOS all ship with voices installed, and browsers add their own. That is why the voice list looks different on every device — a phone may offer a handful, a desktop may offer dozens, and the quality varies with the voice.

Because the speech is generated on your device, nothing is uploaded and nothing is recorded. There is no account, no usage limit and no cost: the feature is part of the browser, and this page is just a friendly way to use it. The trade-off is that you get the voices your device has, not a studio catalogue — but for listening to an article or checking how a sentence sounds, they are more than good enough.

Listening to your own writing

Hearing your words read aloud is one of the best editing tricks there is. Awkward sentences, repeated words and missing words all jump out when you listen, even when your eyes have read the same paragraph ten times and missed them. Writers, students and professionals use text to speech to proofread essays, emails and reports before sending them.

It helps with accessibility too. Long articles become listenable, tired eyes get a rest, and people who absorb information better by ear can listen instead of reading. Adjust the speed to match your comfort — a little faster than normal speech is often easier to follow for long text, while a slower speed suits careful proofreading.

What this tool cannot do

This tool plays speech aloud and that is all it does: there is no audio file to download, because the browser speaks the text in real time and does not record it. Saving speech as an MP3 needs a server-side service that generates and stores an audio file, which is a different kind of tool entirely — and one that would need your text sent to a server.

If you need an audio file, a screen recorder can capture the sound while the tool speaks, or a dedicated text-to-speech service can generate one. But for listening — proofreading, learning a pronunciation, resting your eyes — this tool does the job in the browser, privately, and without asking you to sign up for anything.

Frequently asked questions

Can I download the speech as an MP3?

No. This tool plays speech aloud in your browser using your device voices, and the browser does not record it. Saving audio as a file needs a server-side text-to-speech service.

Why do I only see one or two voices?

Voices come from your device and browser, so the list varies. Phones usually offer fewer voices than desktops, and installing extra voices in your system settings adds them to the list.

Does the voice quality depend on my device?

Yes. The speech engine and voices are built into your operating system and browser, so a newer device usually sounds more natural than an older one.

Is there a limit on how much text I can hear?

No fixed limit, but very long texts can be slow to start and some browsers handle long utterances less reliably. For long articles, paste a section at a time.

Is my text uploaded anywhere?

No. This tool runs entirely in your browser using JavaScript. Your text is never uploaded, stored or sent anywhere — everything stays on this device.

Can I change how fast the text is read?

Yes — the rate control speeds the speech up or slows it down, and the pitch control raises or lowers the voice. Slowing down helps when you are proofreading, and speeding up helps when you are listening to a long article.

Can I use text to speech for proofreading?

Yes — hearing a draft read aloud is one of the best ways to catch mistakes your eyes skip: repeated words, missing words and sentences that run too long. Paste the draft, press play, and listen for the places where the speech stumbles.

What can I listen to with text to speech?

Any text you paste: an article, a draft, an email, a passage from a book. It is made for proofreading, for resting your eyes, and for hearing how a word is pronounced — paste the text and press play.
