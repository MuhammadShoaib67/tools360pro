When you need the sound without the picture

A video file is mostly picture — often 95% of its size. Plenty of the time the picture is the part you do not need: a lecture you want to listen to on a walk, a podcast recorded as video, a wedding song trapped inside a phone recording, an interview you need as audio notes. Extracting the MP3 keeps the part that matters in a file that plays everywhere — every phone, car stereo and music app on earth speaks MP3 — and typically shrinks it to a tenth of the size.

Two things are worth knowing before you convert. First, match the bitrate to the content, not to your hopes: speech stays perfectly clear at 128 kbps, while music deserves 192 or 320. Second, re-encoding cannot invent quality — if the original video's sound was quiet or tinny, the MP3 will faithfully keep it that way. And because everything here happens in your browser tab's memory, shorter everyday clips convert fastest; for a two-hour recording, split it first and convert the parts.

Frequently asked questions

Is my video uploaded to a server?

No. Your browser reads the file, extracts its sound and builds the MP3 on your own device. The file never leaves your computer or phone, and nothing is stored anywhere.

Which files can I convert?

MP4, WebM and MOV videos, plus M4A, MP3, WAV and OGG audio files. The browser itself decodes the sound, so a file only fails if your browser cannot play it — MKV files and videos with unusual audio codecs (like AC3) are the common exceptions.

Which bitrate should I choose?

128 kbps is plenty for speech, lectures and voice notes. 192 kbps is a good all-round choice for most videos. 320 kbps keeps the most detail for music — but it cannot add quality the original file never had.

How large a file can I convert?

The conversion happens in your tab's memory, so everyday clips work best — up to about 100 MB or 30–60 minutes of audio is comfortable on most devices. Very long recordings may run out of memory; splitting them into parts avoids that.

Will the MP3 sound as good as the video?

Very close. The sound is re-encoded, which always loses a tiny amount of detail — pick 320 kbps to keep the loss smallest. The picture is discarded, of course: an MP3 carries sound only.
