How extractive summarising works

An extractive summarizer does not read the way a person does — it counts. First it splits your text into sentences, then it counts how often each meaningful word appears. Words that repeat are usually the topic: in an article about photosynthesis, "light" and "chlorophyll" appear far more often than "however" or "table". Each sentence is then scored by the words inside it, with a small bonus for the opening and closing sentences, where writers usually put their main point and their conclusion.

The top-scoring sentences become the summary, kept in their original order so the result still reads like the text it came from. Because every sentence is copied word-for-word, nothing can be invented or misquoted — the worst a summary can do is miss a nuance, never fabricate one. That is the trade-off against tools that rewrite text in their own words and sometimes get the details wrong: extractive summaries are always faithful, and always traceable back to the source.

Getting a good summary

The summary is only as good as the text you paste. Clean copy works best: remove navigation menus, cookie banners and "related articles" lists before pasting, because stray fragments dilute the word counts and can push a junk sentence into the summary. If the article has headings, keep them — they usually repeat the key terms and reinforce the scoring. Very short texts (under five sentences) do not need a summarizer at all; the tool will simply return the whole thing.

Use the length setting to match the job. Short (3 sentences) is right for a quick "what is this about" check before deciding whether to read the full article. Medium (5 sentences) suits revision notes and briefings. Detailed (8 sentences) keeps the supporting points too, which helps when you need the argument, not just the conclusion. And always read the summary against the original before quoting from it — a summary tells you what matters, but only the source tells you exactly what was said.

Frequently asked questions

What does “extractive” mean?

It means the summary is built from sentences taken word-for-word from your own text — the tool ranks each sentence by importance and keeps the strongest ones. It never writes new sentences, never paraphrases, and uses no AI, so nothing in the summary was invented.

Is there a word limit?

No. Paste a paragraph or a whole chapter — the summarizer works on any length, because everything runs in your browser and nothing is sent to a server.

What do Short, Medium and Detailed mean?

They set how many sentences the summary keeps: Short keeps 3, Medium keeps 5 and Detailed keeps 8. The slider lets you pick any number from 1 to 10, and moving it overrides the preset.

Does it work on any text?

It works best on structured prose — articles, essays, reports and study notes, where the key ideas sit in full sentences. Lists, dialogue and poetry summarise poorly, because their meaning is spread across fragments rather than sentences.

Is my text uploaded anywhere?

No. The summary is calculated by JavaScript in your browser. Your text never leaves your device, and nothing is stored.

How does the summarizer choose which sentences to keep?

It counts words: sentences are scored by how often their meaningful words appear in the text, with a small bonus for the opening and closing sentences. The top-scoring sentences become the summary, kept in their original order.

Can I summarize a long article?

Yes — there is no word limit, so paste the whole article. Pick Detailed (8 sentences) or use the slider to choose up to 10 sentences when the piece is long and you need more than the core point.

Does this tool use AI to write the summary?

No. It counts words and scores sentences — an extractive method, not AI writing. Every sentence in the summary is copied word-for-word from your text, so nothing can be invented or misquoted.
