Why meetings cost more than they look

A one-hour meeting with eight people paid 50 an hour costs 400 — and no invoice ever arrives, so nobody sees the number. The cost is real all the same: those eight hours of attention could have been spent on anything else, and the meeting consumed them. Putting a price on a meeting is the fastest way to decide whether it is worth having at all.

The number this calculator shows is the direct cost only: people multiplied by rate multiplied by time. The true cost is higher, because meetings also cost preparation time, travel time, and the minutes it takes to refocus afterwards. Treat the result as a floor, not a ceiling — if the floor already looks expensive, the real price is worse.

The meeting cost formula

The maths is simple: total cost equals the number of attendees times the average hourly rate times the duration in hours. Five people at 40 an hour for one hour is 5 times 40 times 1, which is 200. For a 90-minute meeting, the duration is 1.5 hours, so the same group costs 300.

The only judgement call is the hourly rate. A plain salary rate gives a conservative figure. A loaded rate — salary plus benefits, office costs and other overheads — gives a fuller picture, and is often 1.5 to 2 times the plain rate. This calculator uses whatever rate you enter, so pick the one that matches the question you are asking.

How to use the number

The number is most useful before a meeting is scheduled, not after. A 30-minute meeting with four people costs a quarter of a 60-minute meeting with eight — same topic, very different price. Cutting the attendee list to the people who actually need to decide, and cutting the default hour to 30 minutes, are the two cheapest savings in any organisation.

It also settles the question of whether a meeting should exist at all. If the answer could be an email, a shared document or a five-minute call, the meeting is the expensive option. Calculate the cost, compare it with the decision the meeting is supposed to produce, and let the number argue for you.

Frequently asked questions

What hourly rate should I enter?

The average hourly cost of the people attending. A plain salary rate gives a conservative figure; adding benefits and overheads gives a fuller loaded rate. The calculator uses the number you enter, so the result is only as honest as the input.

Does it count preparation time?

No. It counts the time people spend in the meeting itself. Preparation, travel and the time it takes to refocus afterwards are real costs too, so the true cost is higher than the number shown.

Can I use it for recurring meetings?

Yes. Calculate one meeting, then multiply by the number of times it repeats. A weekly one-hour meeting with six people costs fifty-two times the single-meeting figure over a year.

What if attendees earn different amounts?

Use the average hourly rate across the group. For a rough estimate that is accurate enough; for an exact figure, calculate each group separately and add the totals.

Is my data uploaded anywhere?

No. The calculation runs entirely in your browser using JavaScript. Nothing you enter is uploaded, stored or sent anywhere.

How do I work out the cost of a meeting?

Multiply the number of attendees by the average hourly rate by the duration in hours. This calculator does that for you and adds the cost per person and the cost per minute — enter the three numbers and the breakdown appears at once.

Why is the result called a floor, not a ceiling?

Because it counts only the direct cost — people, rate and time in the room. Preparation, travel and refocus time are real costs on top, so the true cost of a meeting is always higher than the number shown here.

What is the cheapest way to cut meeting costs?

Fewer people and less time: the cost is people times rate times duration, so halving the attendees or halving the length halves the cost. A 15-minute meeting with three people costs a fraction of an hour-long meeting with ten.
