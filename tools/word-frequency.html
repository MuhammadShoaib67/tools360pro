What word frequency tells you about a text

Counting how often each word appears is the simplest way to see what a piece of writing is really about. The most frequent content words are usually the topic: a recipe repeats ingredients and verbs like mix and bake, a report repeats the names of the things it discusses, and a story repeats its characters. If the top of the list surprises you, the text may be drifting away from its intended subject.

Frequency also exposes habits. Writers repeat favourite words without noticing — really, just, very, actually — and a frequency table makes those tics visible in seconds. Editors use it to spot repetition before publishing, students use it to check whether an essay stays on topic, and language learners use it to find the words worth learning first, because the most common words in a text are the ones that matter most to understanding it.

How this counter handles words

A word here is a run of letters or numbers, optionally joined by an apostrophe or a hyphen, so don't and well-known count as single words. Punctuation and symbols are ignored, and numbers on their own count as words too, which matters when you are analysing text full of figures. By default the count is case-insensitive, so The and the add up together — untick Ignore case if capitalisation matters to you.

The Ignore common words option removes a list of about ninety everyday English words — articles, pronouns, prepositions and helper verbs — so the table shows the words that carry meaning. It is the quickest way to get a sense of a long document: paste a report, tick the box, and the top ten words are usually a fair summary of what it is about.

Word frequency and search optimisation

Search engines have used word frequency for decades as one small signal of what a page is about, which is why SEO tools report keyword density — the same idea, expressed as a percentage. A page about coffee should mention coffee more than any unrelated word, but there is no magic percentage to aim for: forcing a keyword into every sentence reads badly and search engines now understand topics through context, not just repetition.

The useful check is a sanity check, not a target. If your main keyword is nowhere near the top of the frequency table, the page probably does not make its subject clear. If it dominates every paragraph, the writing may feel stuffed and repetitive. Somewhere in between — the keyword clearly present, surrounded by related words — is where good pages sit. This counter gives you the numbers; your judgement decides what they mean.

Frequently asked questions

What counts as a word in this tool?

A run of letters or numbers, optionally joined by an apostrophe or a hyphen, so hyphenated words and contractions count as one word each. Punctuation and symbols are ignored, and standalone numbers count as words.

Why does the table show only the top 100 words?

Very long texts can contain thousands of unique words, and a table of all of them would be slow to render and hard to read. The top 100 covers the words that matter, and the copy button still copies the full list.

Does Ignore common words remove words from the total?

No. The total words figure always counts every word in your text. The option only hides common filler words from the table itself, so the meaningful words stand out.

Is my text uploaded anywhere?

No. This tool runs entirely in your browser using JavaScript. Your text is never uploaded, stored or sent anywhere — everything stays on this device.

Can I use this for languages other than English?

Yes, for the counting itself: the tool recognises letters from any alphabet, so it works with French, Urdu written in Roman letters, German and many others. The Ignore common words list is English only, so leave that option off for other languages.
