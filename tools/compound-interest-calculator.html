The snowball that rewards patience

Compound interest is the reason starting early beats starting big. Because each period's interest itself starts earning, growth is not a straight line but a curve that steepens — in the example defaults above, notice how much of the final total arrives in the last few years rather than the first. The year-by-year table makes the snowball visible: deposits stay the same, but the interest column quietly overtakes them.

Play with the levers to feel their weight. Doubling the years usually beats doubling the deposit; a couple of points of rate matter more than a slightly larger starting amount; and the monthly deposit — boring as it sounds — is the lever fully in your control. Remember the result is the clean maths version: real accounts add tax, fees and inflation on top, which the FAQ above shows how to approximate.

Frequently asked questions

What is compound interest, in plain words?

Interest on top of interest. Each period your balance earns interest, and the next period that bigger balance earns interest too — so growth accelerates over time instead of staying flat.

How often should interest compound?

Use whatever your account actually does: savings accounts commonly compound monthly or daily, while many investments are shown yearly. More frequent compounding grows slightly faster at the same rate.

Does this account for tax or inflation?

No — it shows the headline growth before tax, fees and inflation. For a rough "real" return, you can lower the interest rate by the inflation rate (for example 8% growth with 3% inflation ≈ use 5%).

Is this financial advice?

No. It is a maths illustration using the numbers you type. Real products vary, markets move, and big decisions deserve a qualified adviser.
