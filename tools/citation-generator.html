Why citing properly matters

Every university and journal insists on a citation style because it makes your work checkable: a reader can find the exact source you used, and you get credit for building on real research instead of hiding it. APA is the standard in psychology, education and the social sciences; MLA rules in literature and the humanities, Chicago is the standard in history and much of publishing, Harvard (Cite Them Right) is the default at many UK universities, and IEEE and Vancouver number their sources for engineering and medicine. The fiddly part is never the idea — it is the punctuation: where the year goes, which title is italic, and when "et al." appears. That is exactly what this tool does for you, across seven styles and ten source types.

One honest warning: generators handle standard sources well, but graded work deserves a final human check. Compare your reference list against your institution's own guide, especially for unusual items like edited chapters, archival material or sources with many authors. And never cite a source you have not actually read — a perfect format cannot rescue a reference that does not support your argument.

Seven styles, one form

The seven styles fall into three families. APA, MLA, Chicago (author-date) and Harvard are author-date styles: the in-text citation names the author and year, and the reference list is alphabetical. IEEE and Vancouver are numbered styles: sources are numbered in the order you cite them, and the in-text citation is just the number in brackets — which is why their lists stay in the order you add them instead of sorting alphabetically. Turabian uses footnotes: the bibliography looks like Chicago's, and the box below the reference shows the footnote form, with the author's first name first.

Pick the style your institution asks for, not the one that looks simplest — a marker checks the style first and the punctuation second. If your guide says "Harvard", it almost always means Cite Them Right, the version this tool follows; if it says "Vancouver", check whether your school wants the reference list numbered in citation order (the standard) or alphabetically (a common local variation). When in doubt, the safest move is to match a published example from your own department's guide, line for line.

DOI, ISBN and title lookup — and the saved bibliography

Typing every detail by hand is slow and error-prone, so the lookup buttons do it for you. A DOI lookup asks Crossref — the registry that issues DOIs — for the article's title, authors, journal, volume, issue and pages. An ISBN lookup asks OpenLibrary for the book's title, authors and publisher. A title search lists the five closest Crossref matches, and one click fills the form. If a lookup finds nothing, or the service cannot be reached, you get a plain message and can still type the details yourself — the tool never guesses or invents a detail.

Your reference list lives in this browser's localStorage, so it survives a refresh and is still there tomorrow — but it is not sent to any server, and clearing your browser data clears it too. Download the .docx copy before a deadline, and keep a backup of anything that matters: localStorage is convenient, not archival.

Citing a website without the guesswork

Websites are the most-cited source students wrestle with, because a web page rarely hands you its details neatly: the author hides in a footer, the date is a “last updated” line, and the title in the browser tab is not always the page’s real title. This free citation generator for websites needs no sign-up — pick APA, MLA or Chicago, choose the website type, and fill in what the page actually shows. If the page has a DOI, the lookup fills the whole form for you; if not, type what you can see and leave the rest blank rather than guessing.

The three big styles treat websites differently, and that is where most marks are lost. An APA citation generator formats the reference with the author and year up front and adds the URL at the end; an MLA citation generator leads with the author and puts the title in quotation marks, with the site name in italics; a Chicago citation generator gives you a footnote form as well as the bibliography entry. The tool handles those differences for you — your job is only to check that the details you typed match the page, especially the date, because a “last updated” date and a publication date are not the same thing.

Frequently asked questions

Which citation styles are supported?

Seven: APA (7th edition), MLA (9th edition), Chicago (17th edition), Harvard (Cite Them Right), IEEE, Vancouver and Turabian, for ten source types: websites, books, journal articles, videos, podcasts, films, dissertations, newspaper articles, reports and conference papers. In-text citations are generated too — numbered [1] for IEEE and Vancouver, and a footnote form for Turabian.

Is anything uploaded or stored?

Almost nothing. Citations are formatted by JavaScript in your browser. The only exceptions are the DOI, ISBN and title lookups, which send just that identifier to Crossref or OpenLibrary to fetch the details, and your reference list, which is saved in this browser's localStorage so it survives a refresh — it is never sent to any server.

How do I cite a source with no author?

Leave the author fields empty. Every style moves the title to the front of the citation in that case, and this tool does that automatically.

What if there is no date?

Leave the date empty. APA shows (n.d.) for no date, MLA leaves the date out, Chicago uses your access date for web pages instead, and Harvard shows (no date) — the tool follows each style's rule.

Should I double-check the result?

Yes — always compare against your institution's guide before submitting graded work. This tool covers standard sources; unusual cases (edited books, sources with many authors, archival material) have extra rules.

Can I build a whole reference list?

Yes. Press Add to list for each source — author-date styles stay sorted alphabetically, while IEEE and Vancouver lists stay in the order you added them, because those styles number sources in citation order. The list is saved in your browser, and you can copy it whole or download it as a .docx file.

How do the DOI and ISBN lookups work?

Type a DOI, an ISBN or a title and press the lookup button. The tool asks the public Crossref or OpenLibrary database and fills the form for you. If nothing is found, or the service cannot be reached, you get a clear message and can still type the details by hand — nothing is ever made up.

Which citation style should I use for my paper?

Use the style your institution or publisher asks for — there is no universally “best” one. APA is the standard in psychology, education and the social sciences; MLA rules in literature and the humanities; Chicago is the standard in history and much of publishing; Harvard (Cite Them Right) is the default at many UK universities; IEEE suits engineering and Vancouver suits medicine. When in doubt, check your department’s guide or ask your tutor — a marker checks the style first and the punctuation second.

What is the difference between APA and MLA citations?

Both are author-date styles, but they look different on the page. In APA, the in-text citation gives the author and year, like (Smith, 2023), and the reference list is headed “References”. In MLA, the in-text citation gives the author and page number, like (Smith 42), the list is headed “Works Cited”, and titles of articles sit in quotation marks. This generator formats both — pick the style and it handles the punctuation.

Add each source to your reference list with the “Add to reference list” button, then use the download option to save the whole list as a .docx file, ready to paste into your paper. The list is also saved in your browser, so it is still there if you refresh the page — but download a copy before a deadline, because clearing your browser data clears the saved list too.
