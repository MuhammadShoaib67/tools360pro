  3. The clock at the top always shows the current epoch second, ticking live.

The number hiding inside every log file

Open any server log, API response or database export and you will meet long numbers like 1762272000 where a date should be. That is Unix time: seconds counted from 1 January 1970, UTC. Software loves it because arithmetic is trivial — subtract two timestamps and you know exactly how far apart two events were, in any country, with no time-zone arguments. Humans, sensibly, do not read time that way, which is why this converter exists on every developer's bookmark bar.

The two traps are worth knowing. First, JavaScript and many APIs use milliseconds (13 digits) while Unix tradition uses seconds (10 digits) — mixing them up throws a date into 1970 or the year 56000. Second, a timestamp is not "in" a time zone; it is one instant, and only its display changes. This tool shows the UTC reading, your local reading, the ISO format and how long ago (or until) it is — the four things you actually need when debugging.

Frequently asked questions

What is a Unix timestamp?

The number of seconds that have passed since midnight UTC on 1 January 1970 (the "epoch"). Computers store times this way because one number is easy to save, sort and subtract — humans just cannot read it.

Seconds or milliseconds — which is my number?

Count the digits: 10 digits is seconds, 13 digits is milliseconds. This converter detects it automatically — anything with 12 or more digits is treated as milliseconds.

Why do UTC and my local time differ?

A timestamp is one fixed moment for the whole planet; the clock reading depends on where you stand. The converter shows both the UTC reading and your device's local reading of the same moment.

What dates are supported?

Any date your browser can represent — far past and far future. Negative timestamps (before 1970) also work.
