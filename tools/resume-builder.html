What makes a resume ATS-friendly?

Most larger employers run resumes through an applicant tracking system before a human sees them, and that software is picky in boring ways: it reads single-column text well and struggles with tables, text boxes, graphics, headers and footers. That is why all four templates here are single-column with no tables or images — Compact ATS is the plainest of the four, with standard section headings (Experience, Education, Skills) that parsers recognise, and no colour at all. Fancy two-column designs look good to a person and garbled to a parser; for online applications, plain wins.

The second ATS factor is keywords. The system compares your resume against the words in the job description, so a resume that says “Python” gets through a filter for Python and one that only says “programming” may not. The keyword check below compares your draft against a pasted job description and shows what is missing — add the missing words only where they are true, in your skills or in a bullet that proves them. Inventing experience to match keywords backfires in the interview; an honest resume with the right vocabulary is the aim.

Students: put education first

If your strongest credential is your degree, lead with it. Move Education above Experience with the reorder arrows, and give it the detail employers actually read: the degree and university, expected graduation date, and anything that sets it apart — a strong GPA, relevant coursework, a final-year project. An empty Experience section hurts more than a missing one, so if you have no jobs yet, leave Experience out entirely (delete its entries) and let projects, volunteer work and skills carry the page.

Projects count as experience when they show real work: a website you built, a tool you shipped, a dataset you analysed. Write each one like a job bullet — what you built, with what, and what happened — and put a number in wherever you honestly can (“cut load time by 40%”, “used by 200 students”). Keep the whole resume to one page: the fill meter measures your draft against a standard A4 page, and for a student or fresh graduate one page is the right length.
