What saturation does to a photo

Saturation is the intensity of colour: how red the reds are, how blue the blues are. Raising it makes colours deeper and more vivid; lowering it drains them toward grey. At 0% the image is fully black and white — the same result as the Grayscale Converter, reached from the opposite direction.

Overcast light, haze and indoor bulbs all drain colour from photos, which is why holiday pictures often look duller than the memory. A saturation lift of 120–140% usually restores the scene to how it felt at the time. Food photos, flowers and sunsets benefit most; the colours were there, the light just muted them.

Finding the sweet spot

The risk with saturation is going too far. Skin is the first casualty: at 160% and above, faces turn orange and sunburnt, and the photo starts to look like a filter rather than a photograph. Greens are the second — grass and leaves go neon. If people or foliage look unnatural, pull the slider back until they look like themselves again.

Small moves are the rule. Most photos need no more than 110–130%. And if only one colour is the problem — a red dress that glows, a blue cast from shade — a global saturation change is the wrong tool: it moves every colour at once. For single-colour fixes you need a full photo editor; this tool is for the overall colour strength of the whole picture.

One more place saturation matters is print. Screens show colours brighter than paper does, so a photo that looks vivid on your phone can print dull. A small saturation lift before printing — 110% or so — compensates for the difference, and it is cheaper than reprinting. Just remember that printers also clip the most intense colours, so what looks rich on screen may still flatten on paper.

Saturation, vibrance and filters

You may have seen a “vibrance” slider in other editors. Vibrance is saturation with manners: it boosts the muted colours and leaves the already-strong ones alone, and it protects skin tones. This tool applies plain saturation, which treats every colour equally — simpler, and predictable, but easier to overdo on faces.

Saturation also pairs with contrast. A photo that looks flat usually needs contrast first: raising contrast alone makes colours look richer, and you may find you need less saturation than you expected. Work in that order — brightness, then contrast, then saturation — and each slider does less work, which keeps the result looking natural.

Frequently asked questions

Are my images uploaded to a server?

No. The adjustment runs on a canvas in your browser. Your images never leave your device.

What does 100% saturation mean?

100% is your original image, unchanged. Higher values make colours more intense; lower values mute them. At 0% the image is black and white.

Why do faces look orange when I raise saturation?

Saturation boosts every colour equally, including the reds in skin. Faces show it first. Pull back until skin looks natural — most photos need no more than 110–130%.

What is the difference between saturation and vibrance?

Vibrance boosts only the muted colours and protects skin tones; saturation boosts all colours equally. This tool applies plain saturation, which is simpler and predictable.

Why can't I download in my browser?

This tool applies its effect with canvas filters, which Safari 17 and older do not support. The preview still works, but downloading needs Chrome, Edge, Firefox, or Safari 18+.

What formats can I download?

Keep the original format, or choose JPEG, WebP or PNG. PNG loses nothing; JPG makes the smallest files for photos.
