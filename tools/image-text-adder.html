Captions, labels and titles

Text on an image does the job a caption underneath cannot: it travels with the picture. A photo shared without its caption loses its context; the same photo with the words drawn on it keeps its meaning wherever it goes. That is why tutorials label their screenshots, sellers put prices on product photos, and families date their holiday pictures.

The same idea works for thumbnails and covers. A title drawn on a video thumbnail is read before any description, and a label on a diagram removes the need for a separate legend. Keep the words short — a line of text is read at a glance, a paragraph is not — and let the picture do most of the talking.

Making text readable on any photo

The hardest part of text on a photo is contrast: white text vanishes on a bright sky, black text disappears into a shadow. This tool draws a subtle dark outline around every letter, which keeps the text readable on both light and dark areas of the same photo. White text with that outline is the safest choice for most pictures.

Size matters too. Text that is too small becomes unreadable when the image is shrunk — a thumbnail on a phone shows a fraction of the pixels the full image has. If the picture will be viewed small, go bigger than feels necessary: text that looks slightly large at full size reads perfectly as a thumbnail. And keep the text away from the busiest part of the photo — a plain sky, a wall or an empty corner makes a better background than a crowd.

Text, watermarks and memes

There is a difference between a caption and a watermark. A caption is meant to be read; a watermark is meant to be noticed just enough to claim ownership. This tool is for captions and titles — one clear line of text. Frequently asked questions

Are my images uploaded to a server?

No. The text is drawn on a canvas in your browser. Your images never leave your device.

Can I add more than one line of text?

This tool adds one line at a time. For a second line, download the result and open it in the tool again — each pass adds another line, so you can build up a title and a subtitle.

Why does my text have a dark edge?

The tool draws a subtle dark outline around the letters so the text stays readable on bright photos. Without it, white text would vanish on a light sky and light text would vanish in shadows.

Which font should I choose?

Arial and Verdana are the clearest for captions; Georgia and Times New Roman suit formal images; Impact is the classic meme font. The preview shows each font live, so try a couple before downloading.

Can I use my own font?

No — this tool offers seven standard web-safe fonts that exist on almost every device, so the text renders the same for everyone. Custom fonts would need a font file upload, which this tool does not do.

What formats can I download?

Keep the original format, or choose JPEG, WebP or PNG. PNG keeps transparency and loses nothing; JPG makes the smallest files for photos.

Which fonts can I use?

Seven: Arial, Georgia, Times New Roman, Courier New, Verdana, Trebuchet MS and Impact. Pick the one that suits the photo — Impact for a bold title, Georgia for a softer caption.

Can I move the text anywhere on the image?

No — the text goes in one of 9 preset positions: top, middle or bottom, each in left, centre or right. There is no dragging the text to an exact spot; pick the preset nearest to where the photo has room.

Will the text be readable on a bright photo?

Yes — the text is drawn with a subtle dark outline, which keeps it readable even on a bright background. Picking a contrasting colour helps too: white text on a dark area, dark text on a light one.
