How to use the Text Similarity Checker

  1. Paste your text in the first box, then paste up to 5 sources you want to compare it with — a draft, a classmate's notes, an earlier version of your own work.
  2. Choose a sensitivity: Low finds matching phrases of 3 words, Medium 5 words, High only 8-word matches.
  3. Press Check similarity. The matching passages are highlighted in your text, with a percentage for each source and a short report you can copy.

What a similarity percentage can and cannot tell you

A similarity percentage is a measurement of overlap between two texts you provide — nothing more. A high percentage against a source you pasted means the two texts share long word-for-word passages, which is worth a look: it might be a quote you forgot to mark, a paragraph you copied from your own earlier draft, or a passage you paraphrased too closely. A low percentage means the texts differ in wording, which is what you want when two people write about the same topic independently.

What the number cannot do is judge the wider world. This checker has no web index, so a low score here says nothing about books, journals or websites you did not paste — and a high score against a pasted source is not, on its own, proof of anything: common phrases, technical terms and quoted passages all match by design, which is why the "ignore quoted text" option exists. Use the percentage as a starting point, then read the highlighted passages yourself and decide what, if anything, needs a citation or a rewrite. How the comparison works

The checker breaks your text into word sequences of the sensitivity length — 3, 5 or 8 words — and looks for the same sequences, in the same order, in each source. Matching is case-insensitive and ignores punctuation, so "The quick brown fox" and "the quick brown fox." count as the same sequence, but the same words in a different order do not. The percentage is the share of your words that appear inside at least one matching sequence, counted separately for each source.

That design is deliberate. Single shared words mean nothing — every English text shares "the", "and" and "of" — so the minimum match is a phrase, not a word. Raising the sensitivity to 8 words filters out common expressions and leaves only substantial shared passages; lowering it to 3 words catches shorter echoes, at the cost of more noise. And because everything runs in your browser, the texts you paste are never uploaded anywhere: close the tab and they are gone. Frequently asked questions

How is the similarity percentage calculated?

The checker breaks your text into word sequences of the sensitivity length (3, 5 or 8 words) and looks for the same sequences, in the same order, in each source. The percentage is the share of your words that appear in a matching sequence. Word order matters: the same words in a different order do not count as a match.

Does this check the internet?

No. This checker only compares the texts you paste on this page. It has no web index and cannot find matches in books, journals or websites you have not pasted here — a low score here says nothing about the wider web.

What does the sensitivity setting change?

It changes the minimum matching phrase length: Low looks for matching sequences of 3 words, Medium 5 words, High 8 words. Low finds shorter shared phrases, including common expressions; High only flags longer passages that match word for word.

Why would I ignore quoted text?

Quoted passages are meant to match their source — that is what quotation marks are for. Ignoring them shows the similarity of your own words, without the quotes you deliberately included and cited.

Is my text uploaded anywhere?

No. The comparison runs entirely in your browser, and your text never leaves this device. Nothing is saved, and closing the tab removes everything.

Can I compare my text against more than one source?

Yes — up to 5 sources at once. Paste your text, add each source, and every source gets its own similarity percentage with its matching passages highlighted, so you can check a draft against several references in one run.

Does it check for plagiarism?

No. It compares your text only against the sources you paste, and it never searches the web — so it cannot tell you whether your text matches something online. A high similarity score means your text overlaps a source you provided, not that it is plagiarised.

Can I compare two texts for similarity?

Yes — paste your text and a single source, and the checker shows the similarity percentage between the two, with the matching passages highlighted. Add more sources whenever you want to check against several references at once.
