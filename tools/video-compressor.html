How the compressor works

When you drop a file in, your browser decodes it with the same video decoder that plays videos on every website — that is why the compressor accepts whatever your browser can play: MP4 (H.264), WebM and MOV in most browsers, with small differences between browsers and devices. A file your browser cannot play cannot be compressed here, and the tool will say so plainly instead of failing silently.

Compression is a re-encode. The tool plays your video once through and records it again with the browser's built-in recorder (MediaRecorder) at a lower bitrate: about 5 Mbps at the High preset, 2.5 Mbps at Medium and 1 Mbps at Low, measured at 1080p and scaled down for smaller resolutions. The audio track is carried over at 128 kbps. Because the video is played in real time, compression takes about as long as the video itself — a two-minute video takes about two minutes, and the progress bar shows exactly where it is.

If you pick a smaller resolution, each frame is drawn onto a canvas at the new size before it is recorded — that is what makes the file smaller still, and it is also why a downscaled video is recorded at up to 30 frames per second. At the original resolution the frames go straight from the player to the recorder, so the frame rate is untouched.

Why the download is a WebM file

Browsers can decode many video formats, but the recorder built into the browser writes WebM — there is no MP4 recorder in Chrome, Edge or Firefox, and Safari only gained WebM recording in version 18.4. A compressor that promised an MP4 file would have to ship a large encoder library or send your file to a server to be encoded there, and neither fits a tool that promises your files never leave your device. So the compressor downloads WebM: the format the browser can honestly produce.

WebM plays in Chrome, Edge, Firefox and VLC, and in most modern video players and editors. If the place you are sending the video to only accepts MP4, convert the WebM afterwards in any video app — open it, export or save as MP4, and the compressed video is ready to share anywhere. The size saving survives the conversion, because the video is already compressed by then.

Choosing a quality preset

Start with Medium at the original resolution. It suits most jobs: a phone recording, a screen capture, a clip for a chat or an upload form. The file shrinks a lot and the picture still looks clean on a phone or laptop screen, because phone cameras and screen recorders usually record at a much higher bitrate than the screen can show.

Pick High when the detail matters — a video you will watch on a big screen, or one with fast movement and fine text, where a low bitrate shows as blocky patches. Pick Low when the file has to be as small as possible: an attachment with a size limit, a slow connection, a video that will only ever be watched small. And remember the honest limit: a video that is already heavily compressed — a clip downloaded from a messaging app, for example — has little left to squeeze out, and at the High preset the compressed file can even come out slightly larger than the original. The tool shows both sizes before you download, so you can always try a lower preset instead.

Frequently asked questions

What video formats can I compress?

Any format your browser can play and decode: MP4 (H.264), WebM and MOV in most browsers. If a file will not load, the browser cannot decode that format or codec — try an MP4 file, or open the tool in another browser.

Does the compressed video stay an MP4?

No — the compressed file downloads as a WebM video (.webm). Browsers record WebM, not MP4, so WebM is the format this tool can honestly produce. WebM plays in Chrome, Edge, Firefox and VLC; if you need an MP4 afterwards, convert the WebM in any video app.

How much smaller will my video get?

It depends on the original: a phone recording at a high bitrate shrinks a lot, while a video that is already heavily compressed shrinks little and can even grow slightly at the High preset. The tool shows the original and compressed sizes before you download, so you see the exact saving.

Does compression reduce the quality?

Yes — re-encoding at a lower bitrate loses some detail, and a smaller resolution loses more. High keeps the most quality, Medium is the balance most people want, and Low gives the smallest file. Picking a smaller resolution also caps the frame rate at up to 30 frames per second.

How long does compression take?

About as long as the video itself, plus a few seconds. The tool re-encodes your video in real time, so a two-minute video takes about two minutes. The progress bar shows exactly where it is.

Is my video uploaded anywhere?

No. Your file is decoded, re-encoded and downloaded entirely in your browser. It never leaves your device, and nothing about it is stored or sent anywhere.

Which browsers does the compressor work in?

Chrome, Edge, Firefox and Safari 18.4 or newer — any browser whose recorder can write WebM. Older Safari versions cannot record WebM, so the tool shows a message there instead of a broken download.

Does the compressed video keep its sound?

Yes — if the original has an audio track, it is carried over into the compressed file (Opus audio at 128 kbps). A video with no audio track stays silent.
