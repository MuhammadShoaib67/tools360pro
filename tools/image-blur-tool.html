What blur is actually good for

Blurred images are workhorses in design. A heavily blurred photo makes a calm background for text, because the shapes and colours stay while the distracting detail melts away. App screens, presentation slides and website headers all use this trick — the photo sets the mood, and the words on top stay readable.

A light blur has a different job: it softens. Portraits with slightly harsh skin detail, product photos with a busy backdrop, and screenshots with small imperfections all look gentler with a blur of 2–5 pixels. The image stays recognisable; only the hard edges relax.

Blurred stills also make good video thumbnails and channel art. A sharp photo with text on top fights for attention; the same photo blurred lets the title do the talking. Content creators use this constantly for end screens and title cards, where the background sets the mood and the words carry the message.

Blur for privacy — and its limits

Blur is the standard way to hide a face, a number plate or an address in a photo you are about to share. It works, but only if the blur is strong enough: a light blur over a number plate can sometimes be read by guessing, and text under a weak blur is often recoverable. For anything genuinely sensitive, use a heavy blur — 15 pixels or more — or crop the detail out entirely.

One honest warning: blur is not encryption. A blurred face cannot be “unblurred” by this tool, but a determined person with enhancement software can sometimes sharpen a lightly blurred image. If the detail must never be seen, remove it with cropping or the AI Object Remover instead of relying on blur alone.

Blur strength and file size

Blurred images compress extremely well. A photo full of fine detail is hard for JPG to squeeze; the same photo blurred to soft colour fields compresses to a fraction of the size. That makes blurred backgrounds cheap to use on websites — a blurred hero image can weigh less than a tenth of the original.

The blur applies to the whole image here — there is no brush to blur one area. For selective blur, crop the area you want blurred, blur the crop, and place it back over the original in an editor. For whole-image softening and backgrounds, this tool is the one-step answer.

Frequently asked questions

Are my images uploaded to a server?

No. The blur runs on a canvas in your browser. Your images never leave your device.

What does the blur number mean?

It is the blur radius in pixels: how far each pixel's colour spreads. 5px is a gentle softening; 20px melts the image into soft shapes and colours.

Can I blur just one face or one area?

No — this tool blurs the whole image. For one area, crop it out with the Crop Image tool, blur the crop, and place it back over the original in an editor.

Can a blurred photo be unblurred?

Not by this tool — the download contains only the blurred pixels. But a light blur can sometimes be partly sharpened by enhancement software, so use a heavy blur (15px or more) for anything genuinely sensitive.

Why can't I download in my browser?

This tool applies its effect with canvas filters, which Safari 17 and older do not support. The preview still works, but downloading needs Chrome, Edge, Firefox, or Safari 18+.

What formats can I download?

Keep the original format, or choose JPEG, WebP or PNG. Blurred images compress very well as JPG, so JPG is usually the smallest choice.

How strong should the blur be?

5px is a gentle softening — enough to take the edge off a photo. For a background behind text, go higher until the words read clearly; the live preview shows the result before you download.

Can I blur just one part of a photo?

No — this tool blurs the whole image at once. A face, a number plate or a line of text cannot be selected and blurred on its own; for that you need a selective-blur tool.

Why blur a photo background?

So text on top of it reads clearly. A softly blurred background keeps the photo's mood while removing the detail that competes with the words — the usual trick for slides, headers and social posts.
